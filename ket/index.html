Rare Languages of the Asia-Pacific · Module 23

Ket

кет · деӈ · the middle Yenisei, Siberia

The last survivor of a language family that once covered half of Siberia — and the best candidate anyone has ever found for a language family that crossed into America

Watch the sky: a line is being drawn from the Yenisei to Alaska.

Where and who

Hunters of the middle Yenisei

  • The Ket live in a handful of villages along the middle Yenisei and its tributaries, in the Turukhansk district of Krasnoyarsk Krai — taiga, forty below in winter, and a river that is the only road.
  • Keʼt means "person." The plural is deŋ, "people." Russians long called them Yenisei Ostyaks, a label borrowed from an unrelated neighbouring people.
  • The 2021 census counted 1,088 Ket. Speaker counts range from 153 (2020 census) down to specialists' estimates of fewer than 30, and local reporting of ten to twenty.
  • In 1926, by contrast, 1,225 of 1,428 Ket — around 86 percent — spoke the language. By 1989 it was under half.

Until the 20th century the Ket were nomadic hunters and fishers over a huge area of boreal forest, with so little contact with neighbours that their language has remarkably few loanwords.

A family of one

The rivers remember

Ket is the last living Yeniseian language. It had at least five siblings: Yugh, Kott, Arin, Assan and Pumpokol. Every one of them is gone, most recorded only in word lists taken by 18th-century travellers before they died out.

But the family left a fingerprint across Siberia. Yeniseian words for river — Ket ses, Kott šet — survive inside hundreds of river names over a vast region: from northern Mongolia up to the middle Yenisei, from the Irtysh in the west to the Angara in the east.

Nobody there speaks Yeniseian now. The rivers are still called what Yeniseian speakers called them. Click the map to meet the family.

This is the detective work of toponymy: place names outlive the people who coined them, so a map of river names can show you where a language used to be.

Think like a linguist

A line to Alaska

In 2008, Edward Vajda proposed that Yeniseian is related to the Na-Dene languages of North America — Navajo, Apache, Tlingit, and the Athabaskan languages of Alaska and Canada. It has been called the first proposed link between an Old World and a New World language family that meets the standards of ordinary historical linguistics.

If it holds, it means a single family straddles the Bering Strait: the speakers who stayed in Siberia, and the speakers who kept going.

It is not settled. Plenty of linguists remain unconvinced, and the work continues. What makes it worth your attention is what kind of evidence it rests on. Weigh three pieces yourself.

Language lab

Nothing like its neighbours

  • It has tone. Ket uses pitch to distinguish words — a feature of Chinese or Yoruba, and essentially unheard of among the languages of northern Siberia. Specialists describe four main tones, though whether to call Ket "tonal" at all has been argued over for decades.
  • Its verbs are prefixing. Every language around Ket builds words by adding endings. Ket builds its verbs by stacking pieces at the front, in fixed numbered slots — and the stem itself can be split in half, with other material inserted between the two halves.
  • Its nouns have classes — masculine, feminine and inanimate — and the class shows up on the verb, not just the noun.
  • Linguists call this verb type "templatic polysynthesis," and Vajda notes it has no parallel anywhere else in northern Eurasia. It does have one across the Pacific: in Na-Dene.

Centuries of intermarriage with neighbouring peoples pushed some Ket structures toward the suffixing pattern around it — but the old prefixing verb is still underneath.

Cultural significance

The universe runs north

The Yenisei flows north to the Arctic. In Ket belief that direction is not neutral: the river is the map of the world, and where you are on it tells you what kind of place you are in.

  • Upriver, to the south, lies Tomam, "mother of heat" — warmth, spring, and the birds that return with it.
  • Downriver, to the north, lies Hosedam, mistress of the underworld, who sends illness, bad weather and death and devours souls.
  • Above everything is Es, both "sky" and "god." The sky itself is described in Ket as Es's skin.
  • Shamans fought Hosedam with the help of spirit helpers. Ket belief also held that a person has seven souls, each with its own name.

Notice what this means for the language: direction words in Ket are not abstract. "Downriver" and "north" and "toward death" sit on the same axis.

How people lived

Birch bark, iron and dogs

  • The Ket moved with the seasons: fishing camps in summer, hunting grounds in autumn and winter. The travelling home was a conical tent of poles covered in birch bark, and the travelling boat was a big covered river craft that could carry a family and its gear.
  • Women worked skins — of animals, birds and fish — and birch bark, which became containers, canoes and roofing. Men worked wood, bone and iron: Ket bows, knives and scrapers were known up and down the Yenisei and traded widely.
  • Dogs were partners rather than livestock, valued for hunting and for the household.
  • A blacksmith tradition in the taiga is a clue to deeper history: metalworking and the conical tent point south, toward the Altai and Sayan mountains, where the genetic and toponymic evidence says Yeniseian speakers came from.
Why birch bark?
Boiled and sewn, birch bark is light, waterproof and free, and the taiga is full of it. A people who move eight times a year cannot carry heavy houses — so the house is bark and poles, and the poles can be cut again at the next camp.

Why so few loanwords?
Scattered hunting groups in a huge forest met outsiders rarely. Ket borrowed grammatical habits from neighbouring languages through intermarriage, but remarkably little vocabulary — which is lucky, because it makes the deep history easier to reconstruct.

Where did they come from?
A 2016 genetic study places Yeniseian origins near the Altai Mountains or Lake Baikal, far to the south. River names agree. The Ket of today live at the far northern end of a long migration downriver — and Russian records show that migration was still going on in the 1600s.

Why it matters now

The arithmetic

Ket is not merely endangered. It is the last branch of an entire family. When a language like Spanish loses ground, its family survives in twenty other languages. When Ket goes, Yeniseian goes with it, and a whole independent way of building a human language stops existing.

  • And the Dene–Yeniseian question gets harder to answer, permanently. You cannot run new fieldwork on a language with no speakers.
  • The causes are familiar from across Siberia: collectivization, forced settlement in a few villages, boarding schools in Russian, and an economy with no place for the taiga hunting life.
  • One grim detail of scale: a researcher documenting the Ket verb in 2021 did it through weekly video calls with a single fluent speaker.

What is being done

Songs, grammars, and a conference in Tomsk

  • Alexander Kotusov (1955–2019) was a Ket folk singer and composer who wrote songs in the language — which matters more than it sounds, because a song is a reason to use a language that no longer has a workplace.
  • Ket now has what most small Siberian languages never got: a full descriptive grammar, dictionaries, a Cyrillic alphabet and a school primer produced in the Soviet period, and a steady stream of research publications.
  • Researchers gather at the Dul'zon Readings, named for Andrey Dul'zon, the linguist who first mapped Yeniseian river names across Siberia and proved how far the family once reached.
  • The hard truth is the same one as everywhere in this course: documentation is going well, and transmission to children is not.

Worth remembering: the first published observations of Ket were made by a travelling naturalist, Peter Simon Pallas, in the 1700s. Nearly three centuries of outside study, and the language is down to a few dozen speakers.

What makes Ket worth the trouble?

Every language is a working experiment in how to encode a world. Ket's experiment is unusually different from the ones around it: tone where no neighbour has tone, prefixes where every neighbour uses suffixes, nouns sorted by animacy, verbs that split in half.

And it may be the clue that connects two continents. The question of whether Yeniseian and Na-Dene are one family is a question about human history — who went where, and when — and Ket is the only living witness left on the Siberian side.
