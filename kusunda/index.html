Kusunda

A language from Nepal with no known relatives anywhere on Earth

गेम्येहाक़

Its speakers call themselves the kings of the forest.

Who and where

Kings of the forest

  • The Kusunda live in western and central Nepal, many of them in Dang district. They have long been called Ban Raja, "kings of the forest."
  • Their own name is Mihaq or Gemehaq. Hima Kusunda, a young learner, explains it as "forest" plus "tiger." Their language is Gemehaq gipan.
  • In rural Nepal, the word Kusunda has also been used as an insult meaning "uncivilised." The community has lived with that label for generations.

Life in the forest

Hunters of the night forest

  • Until the mid-1900s, the Kusunda moved through the forests, hunting birds and monitor lizards and gathering yams.
  • They shot birds resting in trees at night with very long, unfeathered arrows, about 160 cm long.
  • Reports describe a silent trade: they left a deer at a farm, expecting farm goods in return.
  • In the 1950s, Nepal took control of large forests. Farming also broke up the jungle. The Kusunda had to settle, and with so few of them left, most married outside the group.

A linguistic surprise

The language that came back

Reference books once listed Kusunda as extinct. Then a trip to get citizenship papers changed everything.

1848

Hodgson describes the Kusunda in print. He later publishes 223 of their words.

1985

The last speaker is reported dead. Kusunda gets listed as extinct.

2004

Three Kusundas visit Kathmandu for citizenship papers. Linguists discover that Gyani Maiya Sen can speak it.

2019

Nepal's Language Commission begins Kusunda classes in Dang.

2025

पिन्डा (Pinda, "The First"), a book of Kusunda writing, is published.

Speak along

A sound from deep in the throat

Kusunda has uvular sounds, made near the little dangling uvula at the back of your mouth. No neighbouring language in the region has them. Let's learn q:

  1. Say "k" as in "kite." Feel your tongue touch the roof of your mouth.
  2. Move that touch further back, toward your uvula.
  3. Say k back there. It sounds deeper, almost like a soft cough. That's q.
  4. Now try it in a word: qasti means "one."

Fun fact: Kusunda vowels usually travel in teams. Words tend to use i, ə, u or e, a, o, but rarely mix them.

"language" · ə sounds like the "a" in "about"

Speak along

Two ways to count to five

Kusunda has traditional numbers from one to five. Pronouns such as tok ("we") use a different set. After five, speakers use Nepali numbers.

Like saying "one, two, three…" out loud.

1qastiQAH-stee
2dukhuDOO-khoo
3dahatdah-HAHT
4piguPEE-goo
5pãgoPAHN-go

Sources spell some numbers differently. The word for "one" also appears as qaːsn. Say ŋ like the "ng" in "sing," and dz like the end of "beds."

A very unusual grammar

A language without "yes" or "no"

Kusunda is reported to have no simple words for "yes" or "no," and no standard way to make an ordinary sentence negative. Context does much of the work.

goraq gina g-əm-u-a

But Kusunda can still refuse!
  • libuŋ ə-go "Play!" · libuŋ ə-nin "Don't play!"
  • otoq means "(that) is not."
  • To refuse tea, you might use "drink" in a form showing it is very unlikely.
  • Kusunda is also reported to lack words for left and right, and for the colour green.

Linguists disagree about parts of this. An earlier grammar described several negative endings.

Hope

Keeping the light on

  • Gyani Maiya Sen helped linguists and early classes until her death in January 2020.
  • Kamala Sen Kusunda was working in a brick kiln when the Language Commission found her. She is now the last fluent native speaker, and she teaches.
  • Hima Kusunda learned the language at school, teaches her elders at home, and wrote a song in Kusunda about life in the forest.
  • Uday Raj Aaley published a Kusunda dictionary in 2017 and leads the classes in Dang.
