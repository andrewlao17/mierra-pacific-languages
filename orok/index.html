Uilta

ульта · Orok · Sakhalin Island, Russia

About three hundred people, fewer than a dozen fluent speakers, and a twentieth century so strange that it has to be explained twice before it makes sense

Watch the snowfield: a reindeer caravan is crossing, and a border is about to appear across its path.

Where and who

The only herders on the island

  • The Uilta live on Sakhalin, a long island off the Russian Far East — the same island as the Nivkh, and once also the Sakhalin Ainu. Three unrelated peoples, three unrelated languages, one island.
  • They split into two groups: the northern Uilta along the Tym river and around the village of Val, who herd reindeer, and the southern Uilta near Poronaysk, who fished.
  • Their language is Tungusic — the family of Evenki, Nanai and historical Manchu — and within it, a close relative of Nanai and Ulch.
  • The 2010 census counted 295 Uilta in Russia. Speaker counts are a whole slide of their own later, but the researchers' figure is now in single digits.

Their own name, Ul'ta or Uilta, is usually derived from ula, "domestic reindeer" — though at least one linguist argues that etymology is unlikely and the name comes from somewhere else entirely.

Nogliki & Poronaysk districts, Sakhalin Oblast, Russia
What made them different

Every other indigenous people of Sakhalin and the lower Amur lived by fishing, hunting and dog teams. The Uilta were the ones with reindeer — used under pack and saddle and harnessed to sleds. One animal gave them a range of movement nobody else on the island had.

In 1925 a count found just over a thousand reindeer among forty-six families. This was never herding on the Siberian scale. It was a small, precise way of living in a hard place.

Cultural significance

One animal, an entire economy

For the northern Uilta, the reindeer was transport, food, clothing and capital all at once. Herds were small and mobile, moving between summer and winter pastures through taiga and bog — country that is almost impassable by any other means.

The language records that life in detail: a vocabulary for herding, for harness, for the condition and age of animals, even — as one linguist noted — terms tracking the months after a reindeer's pregnancy or birth.

History you have to explain twice

A border across the middle of the island

  • After the Russo-Japanese War, Japan took the southern half of Sakhalin. From 1905 to 1945 the island was cut in two at the 50th parallel: Japanese Karafuto in the south, Russia and then the Soviet Union in the north.
  • The Uilta, who had moved with their herds across that country for generations, now lived on a frontier — and both sides noticed how useful people who know the forest can be.
  • Under Japanese rule many indigenous people of Karafuto were concentrated in a managed settlement called Otasu, held up as a showcase of colonial administration.
  • And here is the detail that decided everything: the Karafuto Ainu were granted full Japanese citizenship in 1932. The Uilta and Nivkh were not.

That sounds like paperwork. In August 1945 it became the difference between being evacuated and being left behind.

One life

Gendanu

Daxinnieni Geldanu was born on Sakhalin in 1926 and given the Japanese name Kitagawa Gentarō. He is usually known as Gendanu. He was the adopted son of an Uilta shaman, and he grew up at Otasu being taught that the emperor was to be revered and his own people were backward.

A house for precious things

Jakka Duxuni

  • In 1975 Gendanu founded the Uilta Kyōkai, an association in Abashiri in northern Hokkaido, to campaign for Uilta rights and to keep Uilta tradition going in a country that did not know the Uilta existed.
  • In 1978 he published his autobiography, written with a local high-school teacher, and opened a small museum: Jakka Duxuni, which in Uilta means roughly "a house for keeping precious things."
  • It held Uilta, Nivkh and Sakhalin Ainu objects — a private museum for three displaced peoples, run by the displaced. Kitagawa Aiko co-founded it and led it after his death; in 1995 she went to Tokyo to ask the government for a memorial to the Uilta war dead, and got no reply.
  • Jakka Duxuni closed in 2012 after about 35 years. Its collection moved to the Hokkaido Museum of Northern Peoples in Abashiri, and in 2024 the materials were exhibited together in Tokyo for the first time.
Why a museum?
Because there was nowhere else. The Uilta in Japan were not recognised as an indigenous people, were not counted, and had no pension, memorial or official history. A one-room museum on the Okhotsk coast was the only institution that said: these people exist, and here is the proof.

Why did it close?
For the reason very small institutions close: the people who built it grew old and died, and a community of a few dozen could not replace them. The objects survive in a bigger museum. The thing that ended was a place run by Uilta hands.

What about the pension?
Gendanu applied repeatedly for a veteran's pension. The Japanese government's position was that Uilta and Nivkh had not been eligible for military service and had therefore been recruited illegally — so no compensation was owed. He died in 1984 with none.

Language lab

Words that have to agree

Uilta, like most Tungusic languages, runs on vowel harmony. The vowels divide into two teams, and every vowel in a word — including the endings you add — has to come from the same team.

That means there is no single form for most endings. There is a hard version and a soft version, and the word you attach it to decides which you use. Sort the vowels in the panel and see.

  • Uilta is Southern Tungusic, closest to Nanai and Ulch on the Amur mainland — so its nearest relatives are across the water, not on Sakhalin.
  • Its neighbours on the island, Nivkh and Ainu, are unrelated to it and to each other. Sakhalin packs three separate deep branches of human language into one island.

Harmony also helps explain why Uilta was hard to write with a borrowed alphabet: Russian Cyrillic has no way to show the difference between the two teams, so a new spelling system had to be designed for it.

2008

A language that got its alphabet in 2008

  • For most of the 20th century Uilta had no practical writing system. Soviet alphabet programmes of the 1930s, which gave scripts to dozens of Siberian languages, passed it by — the community was too small to be worth the effort.
  • The person who finally built one was a Japanese linguist, Jirō Ikegami, who had studied Uilta since the 1950s and compiled a dictionary of it.
  • In 2008 the first Uilta primer was published — a Cyrillic-based system, produced with Uilta speakers including Elena Bibikova, with local funding. The title translates roughly as "let's speak Uilta."
  • The language is now taught in one school on Sakhalin.

Think about that sequence. A writing system arrived a century after the colonisation, decades after the gulag, and at a point when the number of fluent speakers had fallen into double digits. It is not too late to be useful. It is extremely late.

Who made Uilta writable?

A collaboration that crossed the same border that broke the community apart: a Japanese linguist who had worked with Uilta speakers who fled to Hokkaido in 1945, and Uilta speakers still living in Russia. The 2008 primer lists both.

Earlier, during the Japanese occupation, a researcher named Nakanome Akira had published a short grammar with about a thousand words — meaning that some of the oldest documentation of this Russian language is in Japanese.

Think like a statistician

Pick a number. Any number.

How many people speak Uilta? Depending on which source you open, the answer is 116, or 64, or 47, or 26, or 10, or 8. These are not sloppy numbers. They are answers to different questions.

A people of three hundred were colonised by one empire, conscripted by its army, imprisoned by the next empire for obeying it, refused a pension by the first, and then built themselves a museum because no country would do it for them.

The language got its alphabet in 2008. It is taught in one school, on an island where fewer than ten people can still speak it properly.
