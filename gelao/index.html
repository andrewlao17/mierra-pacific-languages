Gelao

Kláo · 仡佬语 · Cờ Lao — Guizhou, China & Hà Giang, Vietnam

Around half a million people identify as Gelao. Perhaps three thousand can speak Gelao. That gap — about one in two hundred — may be the widest of any people on earth

Watch the terraces: the rice is ripening toward harvest.

Where and who

The first people of Guizhou

  • The Gelao call themselves Kláo. They are one of China's 56 officially recognised ethnic groups, and they are widely regarded as among the original inhabitants of Guizhou — linked in Chinese records to the ancient Pu and Lao peoples and to the old kingdom of Yelang.
  • Over 90% live in the Zunyi region of northern Guizhou, including two autonomous counties named for them — Wuchuan and Daozhen — where about half the population is Gelao.
  • Smaller communities live in Guangxi, Yunnan, Sichuan and across the border in Hà Giang, Vietnam, where the Cờ Lao are one of the country's smallest recognised peoples.
  • The ethnic population recorded in China's 2000 census was 579,357. The language is a different story entirely.

The Gelao homeland is west-central Guizhou; the communities in Guangxi, Yunnan and Vietnam are the result of later migrations out of that core.

Zunyi, Guizhou, China
Why Guizhou?

Guizhou is a province of limestone karst: steep towers, sinkholes, narrow valleys and very little flat land. For centuries that terrain made it hard to govern from outside, which is a large part of why so many distinct peoples and languages survived here when they did not survive elsewhere.

The same terrain made the province poor, which in the 20th century pushed people toward roads, cities, schools and the language that works in all three: Mandarin.

The gap

A people without its language

Most endangered-language stories are also stories of a shrinking people. Gelao is not. The Gelao are numerous, recognised, and growing. They have autonomous counties, festivals, officially protected status and a seat in China's list of nationalities.

And roughly 99% of them cannot speak Gelao.

  • Estimates of speakers range from about 3,000 to about 7,900, out of more than half a million Gelao. Around 500 were recorded as monolingual in the late 1990s; almost certainly fewer now.
  • Nobody banned it. What happened was slower: Gelao communities are scattered among much larger Han, Miao and Yi populations, Southwestern Mandarin became the language everyone shared, and Gelao had no writing, no schooling and no official role.

This is worth sitting with. An ethnic identity can survive almost completely intact while the language attached to it disappears — which means you cannot measure a language's health by counting the people who claim it.

Inside the name

"Gelao" is not one language

Here is the second surprise. What gets written in censuses as "the Gelao language" is actually a cluster of varieties that are not mutually intelligible. Two Gelao people from different counties may have no shared language except Mandarin.

They are traditionally distinguished by colour names — White, Red, Green — plus a central variety. Click through them, and watch the speaker numbers.

Confusingly, "Red Gelao" refers to two different things: A'ou in Guizhou, and Vandu across the border in Vietnam. Both are down to a few dozen speakers, and they are not the same language.

The family

Distant cousins of Thai

Gelao belongs to Kra–Dai, the family that includes Thai and Lao — national languages with tens of millions of speakers — and Zhuang, the largest minority language in China.

Gelao sits on the Kra branch: one of the family's deepest and most divergent splits, far from the Thai–Lao end. And that is exactly why linguists care so much about it.

  • To reconstruct the history of a language family, you need its oldest branches, not its biggest ones. A thousand Thai speakers tell you less about proto-Kra–Dai than one Gelao elder does.
  • The Kra branch is also where nearly all the endangerment is. The family's giants are thriving. Its deep roots are the part dying.

Gelao also shows exceptional internal diversity — which has made it, in one researcher's framing, a top priority for fieldwork in the whole family.

Cultural significance

The festival with no date

  • The great Gelao festival is 吃新节 — Eating New Rice. It has no fixed date on any calendar. It happens when the first grain ripens, usually in the seventh or eighth lunar month, so the harvest decides.
  • There is a custom in it worth pausing on: during the festival a Gelao person may walk into any family's field — Gelao, Han, Miao or Yi — cut a sheaf of ripening grain for the ancestor offering, and no one will stop them.
  • The Gelao also hold ancestor ceremonies, a Cattle King festival honouring the animals that work the fields, and performances of Nuo masked opera — one of the oldest masked ritual theatre traditions in China.
  • And they revere bamboo. Gelao tradition treats bamboo as ancestral, and it runs through daily life: houses, tools, flutes, ritual objects.

All of this is alive and well. Festivals, masks, medicine and bamboo craft are being documented, funded and promoted. The language is the piece that has not come along with them.

Think like a scholar

The book that wasn't

In 2009 a book surfaced in Guizhou, written — it was claimed — in an ancient Gelao script, recording the deep history of the Gelao people. For a people with no traditional writing system, this would have been extraordinary.

Scholars examined it. It is a forgery.

The interesting question is not "who faked it." It is why this keeps happening. When a community loses its language but keeps its identity, the pressure to produce proof of an ancient heritage becomes enormous — and somebody always obliges.

How it happened

Four ordinary pressures

No single villain explains Gelao. What explains it is four unremarkable facts, each individually reasonable, stacking up over a few generations.

  • Being outnumbered. Gelao villages are scattered among far larger Han, Miao and Yi populations. In a mixed village the shared language wins, and it is never the smallest one.
  • No writing. Gelao was never written. A language with no script is absent from every sign, textbook and document, and so becomes invisible in exactly the places that confer prestige.
  • Internal division. Because the varieties are mutually unintelligible, there was never one "Gelao language" to standardise, teach or broadcast. Even a well-funded revival would have to choose which Gelao.
  • Mobility. Roads, schools and work in the cities all run in Mandarin, and a family that wants its children to do well chooses accordingly. That is not a failure of loyalty; it is a rational decision made half a million times.
Could it be reversed?
Partly — but the arithmetic is brutal. With fewer than 50 speakers in most varieties, almost all elderly, a full revival would require teaching materials for several separate languages, trained teachers, and a reason for children to use it. The honest assessment of researchers is that fieldwork is urgent precisely because revival may not be possible for most varieties.

Does recognition help?
It helps the culture enormously — the autonomous counties, festival funding and heritage listings are real. It has not yet saved the language, which shows that official status for a people is not the same thing as support for a language.

What about Vietnam?
The Cờ Lao of Hà Giang number only a few thousand, and across all three Gelao varieties there, one estimate put the total at about 350 speakers. Red Gelao (Vandu) is down to roughly 50 — one of the most endangered languages anywhere in Southeast Asia.

What is being done

A race measured in elders

  • Chinese linguists have done the heavy lifting. Zhang Jimin published a study of Gelao in 1993; Li Jinfang has led surveys of endangered languages across southwest China and studied the linguistic ecology of the Gelao people.
  • International work has run alongside it: Jerold Edmondson documented Red Gelao, the most endangered form, and reconstructed parts of proto-Kra; later researchers have mapped the varieties and recorded audio word lists.
  • The scale of what is left is stark. One study reported just three speakers of Shuicheng Gelao. A single elderly speaker of one Red Gelao dialect was located in 2013, and that variety is now probably gone.
  • Meanwhile Gelao culture is thriving and visible: festivals, Nuo opera, traditional medicine, tourism in Wuchuan and Daozhen. Both things are true at the same time.

That split — culture rising while language falls — is the thing to take away from this module. They are not automatically connected, and assuming they are is how a language gets lost while everyone is celebrating.
