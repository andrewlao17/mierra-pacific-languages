Liki

An island language of Papua, Indonesia, also called Moar

Field notes: one of the least-documented languages on Earth…

Where and who

Two small islands

  • Liki is spoken on Liki and Nirumoar, small islands off the north coast of Sarmi, in Papua province, Indonesia.
  • About 320 people are Liki.
  • Its neighbourhood is crowded with languages: the Sarmi area alone has 23 Papuan and 11 Austronesian languages.
  • For everyday life, most people now use Papuan Malay, and Indonesian at school and work.

How endangered?

Eleven speakers

native speakers (11, in 2005)Liki people (about 320)
  • Today's active speakers are of the grandparent generation or older. Children are no longer learning it.
  • Why? Researchers point to people moving to towns, and the pull of a powerful national language.

NEARLY EXTINCT

A huge family

At the western edge of the Oceanic family

  • Liki is an Oceanic language, part of the great Austronesian family.
  • Its Oceanic relatives stretch across the Pacific: Fijian, Samoan, Māori, Hawaiian, even Rapa Nui on Easter Island.
  • Liki's little branch, the Sarmi–Jayapura languages, sits at the far western edge of that whole family.

One word, many islands

About 3,000 years ago, the ancestor language Proto-Oceanic had a word for "bird": *manuk. Its descendants still use it across thousands of kilometres of ocean. Say them together!

bird: Wakde (near Liki) · Māori (New Zealand) · Hawaiian (Hawai‘i)

The bigger picture

A land of languages

Of the languages of Indonesian New Guinea:

about 1 in 5 is Austronesian, like Likiabout 4 in 5 are Papuan languages
  • Around Sarmi alone, several languages are already shifting to Papuan Malay, and a few are nearly gone.
  • When a language with a handful of speakers disappears, the world loses a whole way of naming places, plants, sea life and family.
