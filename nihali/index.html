Nihali

India's mystery language, buried under layers of borrowed words

Grab a shovel. We're digging down to words nobody can trace.

Who and where

A people with more than one name

  • Nihali is spoken in a few villages in Buldhana district, Maharashtra, in the foothills of the Satpura mountains, south of the Tapti River.
  • Outsiders say Nihal and Nihali. Speakers call themselves kalʈo and their speech kalʈo manɖi.
  • Careful: a different, unrelated language is also called Kalto or Nahali. Even researchers have mixed them up!
  • All speakers also use other languages, such as Korku, Marathi or Hindi. UNESCO lists Nihali as critically endangered.

Case file

The language that kept its secrets

Linguist Norman Zide reported that Nihali was used as a private language, and that some early researchers were turned away or misled. Scholars spent decades trying to work out what it was.

1906

A British survey groups Nihali with the Munda languages, the family of nearby Korku.

1941

Robert Shafer argues that it has a separate origin.

1957

After fieldwork, Bhattacharya says it likely came from a family now lost.

1962

F. B. J. Kuiper studies 505 word roots. He finds layers from many sources, and about 123 he cannot explain.

2014

K. S. Nagaraja publishes a grammar, stories and vocabulary list.

STILL UNSOLVED Today it is treated as a language isolate, though proposed links to languages such as Kusunda and Ainu remain unproven.

Speak along

Words from the core

These words have no clear match in nearby languages. First, learn one sound: the dotted ṭ in corṭo, "blood."

  1. Say a normal "t". Notice your tongue tip behind your top teeth.
  2. Curl your tongue tip back, toward the roof of your mouth.
  3. Say t from there. It sounds darker and heavier. That's ṭ.
  4. Now try cor-ṭo. The letter c sounds like "ch" in "church."

Tip: a ː after a vowel means hold it longer. Pronunciations here are approximate.

name

Also try puar, "good," and beṭhe, "not."

Life and stories

Their own way of telling it

  • Nihali speakers are believed to have once been forest hunters and foragers. Today most work as farm labourers.
  • Researcher Shailendra Mohan found they have their own version of the Panchatantra, India's famous animal fables.
  • He reported that they sing Ramayana songs but honour Ravana, the story's usual villain, as their hero.
  • Pressure is high: people need other languages for work, many marry outside the community, and much of the old vocabulary has already gone. Many Nihals in Nimar have shifted to Korku or other languages.

Hope

Bringing words to the surface

Between 2011 and 2013, Shailendra Mohan and speakers including Vijay Yadav and Bhawram recorded about 20 hours of stories, songs, history and everyday talk.
