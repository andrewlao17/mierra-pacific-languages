Shompen

Shom Peng · interior of Great Nicobar Island, India

Spoken by a few hundred people who have mostly chosen not to meet us — which makes this the shortest entry in the world's language catalogues, and one of the most urgent

Watch the right-hand edge of the screen.

Where and who

An island between two countries

  • The Nicobar Islands sit in the Indian Ocean between India and Thailand. They belong to India; the nearest Indonesian island is closer than the nearest Indian city.
  • On the coasts live the Nicobarese, farmers and fishers numbering in the tens of thousands. In the forested interior of the southernmost island live the Shompen, in small mobile bands along the rivers.
  • Counts range from about 200 to 400. India classifies them as a Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group, and most Shompen have little or no regular contact with outsiders.
  • Not all of them: one group on the east coast trades with coastal Nicobarese, and in 2014 a polling station was set up so some Shompen could vote for the first time.

They are neighbours, 150 km south, of the Sentinelese — the people of North Sentinel Island, who have refused contact entirely.

Great Nicobar, Andaman & Nicobar Islands, India
Why this module is different

Every other language in this course has a "say it out loud" slide. This one does not, and that is deliberate. The entire published record of Shompen is a handful of word lists collected by outsiders, some of them over a century old and of uncertain accuracy.

We are not going to teach you to pronounce words we cannot verify, from a people who did not ask to be studied. What we can do is be precise about what is known, what is argued, and what nobody outside the island knows at all.

The family

Cousins of Vietnamese

Shompen belongs — almost certainly — to Austroasiatic, one of the world's great language families. Its members stretch from Vietnam and Cambodia across Thailand and Myanmar into eastern India, and out to these islands.

That family contains Vietnamese, spoken by around 85 million people. It also contains Shompen, spoken by a few hundred. Click the bars to compare.

Shompen and Nicobarese are the family's westernmost outpost — the end of a very long journey from mainland Southeast Asia, made by people in boats, probably thousands of years ago.

Genetics supports the link: every one of twelve Shompen men sampled in one study carried a Y-chromosome marker associated with the spread of Austroasiatic-speaking populations.

The argument

Where does Shompen go on the tree?

"Almost certainly Austroasiatic" is doing real work in that sentence. Scholars have put Shompen in three different places, and the disagreement has not been settled.

The problem is the data. The word lists are thin and partly unreliable, and Shompen has clearly borrowed a lot from Nicobarese — so when a Shompen word matches a Nicobarese word, you cannot tell whether it was inherited or borrowed. That single ambiguity is the whole dispute.

  • There is one clue nobody expected: Shompen contains Portuguese and Malay loanwords. A people described as uncontacted have words that arrived by sea centuries ago.
  • "Uncontacted" has never meant "sealed off." It means a people who have decided how much contact they want.

How people live

The forest is the economy

  • The Shompen are semi-nomadic hunter-gatherers, moving in small bands through the island's interior, along the rivers that run down from the forested hills.
  • They live on what the forest and the rivers give: wild pandanus fruit, which is pounded into a paste and is a staple; fish; honey; and pigs, which they also keep. Some groups do a little planting.
  • Travel is by river. The rainforest interior of Great Nicobar has no roads worth the name, which is exactly why the Shompen have been able to live there on their own terms.
  • Bands are small and scattered, which has consequences: genetic studies have found evidence of a very small gene pool, and an unusual pattern of blood groups found nowhere else.

Everything in this list comes from outsiders' observations — anthropologists, officials, occasional visitors. The Shompen have not written an account of themselves, and nobody has asked them to.

Why does the forest matter so much?
A hunter-gatherer economy needs a very large area per person. You cannot compress it into a reserve the way you can compress a farm. If a third of the island is built over, the Shompen do not lose a third of their livelihood — the remaining land has to feed everyone, and it cannot.

Why do rivers matter?
The rivers are the roads, the fishery and the boundary markers between bands. Projects that dam, divert or silt up rivers do not look like attacks on a people. They work like one.

What about the 2004 tsunami?
The Boxing Day tsunami devastated the Nicobars and reshaped the coastal communities. The Shompen, living inland and uphill, largely survived — one of the few times that being remote and unhelped turned out to be an advantage.

Why it matters now

The arithmetic of a mega-project

India has approved a development plan for Great Nicobar: an international transshipment port, an airport, a power plant, a defence presence and a new township. Government documents describe a planned population of around 650,000 people and roughly a million visitors a year.

Around a third of the island falls inside the project area, and more than half of that lies within the official Tribal Reserve. Estimates of the cost have risen from about $1 billion in 2020 to over $10 billion by 2025.

The strategic logic is real and worth understanding: Great Nicobar sits beside the Strait of Malacca, one of the busiest shipping lanes on earth, and India currently has no deep-water transshipment port of its own on that route.

The argument in public

Two questions: consent, and immunity

  • Consent. Indian and international law require the free, prior and informed consent of Indigenous peoples for projects on their land. The Tribal Council gave consent in August 2022 — and later said that consent had been misrepresented. In 2025 India's Tribal Affairs Ministry asked the island administration to explain, and the Council confirmed the dispute.
  • Immunity. Peoples with little outside contact have little resistance to common infections. This is not speculation; it is the documented history of the Great Andamanese, of Enggano, and of the Americas. In February 2024, 39 genocide scholars from 13 countries wrote to India's President calling the project a death sentence for the Shompen. Indian experts on the region wrote a similar letter in April 2024.
  • The government has continued with clearances. More than 100 former Indian civil servants have publicly objected.

Your job here is not to agree with a slide. It is to be able to state both cases accurately, then decide.

Make the strongest case for each side:

For the project

  • India has no deep-water transshipment port on the Malacca route; cargo is handled in Singapore and Colombo instead.
  • Strategic position in the Bay of Bengal, with a defence rationale.
  • Jobs, connectivity and services for a remote territory.
  • A national government argues it is elected to make decisions of national scale.

Against the project

  • Contact and disease could kill a people of a few hundred with no immunity.
  • Over half the project area lies in a legally protected Tribal Reserve.
  • Consent is disputed by the Tribal Council itself.
  • Hundreds of thousands of trees in a biodiversity hotspot; costs have risen tenfold.

Discuss: is there a version of this project that could be built without the harm? If yes, describe it. If no, say what that implies.

The language stake

A question that would close forever

Every language in this course would be a loss. Shompen would be an unusual kind of loss, because the thing that disappears is a question we have not answered yet.

  • We do not have a grammar of Shompen. We do not have a dictionary. We do not have recordings of connected speech, stories, songs or conversation. There is nothing to fall back on.
  • Compare it with Pazeh in Taiwan: its last native speaker died in 2010, but a dictionary, a song collection and video recordings survive her. For Shompen, that archive does not exist.
  • And the obvious solution — send in linguists — is not simple. Fieldwork means contact, contact means disease, and the people in question have not asked for either.

That is the genuine dilemma at the centre of this module, and it does not have a tidy answer. The one thing that is clear is that the choice belongs to the Shompen, and that a port built without asking them takes the choice away.
