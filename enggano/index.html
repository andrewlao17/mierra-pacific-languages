Rare Languages of the Asia-Pacific · Module 24

Enggano

Pulau Enggano · Bengkulu, Indonesia

An Austronesian language so thoroughly disguised that serious linguists argued, for forty years, that it belonged to no known family at all

Watch the horizon: the island is coming out of the mist.

Where and who

The last of the Barrier Islands

  • A chain of islands sits off the west coast of Sumatra, out in the Indian Ocean: Simeulue, Nias, Mentawai — and, at the southern end, Enggano. Four hundred square kilometres, nowhere higher than 281 metres, six villages on one road.
  • Around 4,500 people live there now, but most are migrants from Sumatra, Java and elsewhere. Estimates of Enggano speakers range from about 700 to about 1,500, and children are growing up speaking Indonesian.
  • The language is endangered and the trend is going the wrong way — but it is also, for linguists, one of the great puzzles of the Austronesian world.
  • When Europeans first reached the island, Enggano culture looked less like Sumatra and more like the Nicobar Islands, a thousand kilometres away — right down to round beehive-shaped houses.

Being low and exposed has consequences. Enggano would have been hit hard by the tsunami from the 1883 Krakatoa eruption.

Enggano, North Bengkulu Regency, Indonesia
Six villages, one road

Meok, Apoho, Malakoni, Kaana, Kahyapu and Banjarsari, all strung along the northeast coast. Every one of them now contains Enggano speakers and speakers of Bengkulu Malay, Minangkabau, Javanese, Sundanese and Buginese. Every Enggano speaker alive is bilingual.

That is the ordinary shape of language loss: not a dramatic ban, just a village where the shared language is somebody else's.

The puzzle

A family that left no fingerprints

To work out which family a language belongs to, linguists start with basic vocabulary — eye, house, five, child, water. Those words change slowly, so related languages usually keep recognisable versions of them.

Malay keeps about 59% of the words reconstructed for the ancient Austronesian ancestor. Enggano keeps about 21% — 46 words out of 217.

That is so low that in 1982 Arthur Capell proposed Enggano was a language isolate that had merely borrowed Austronesian words, and in 2014 Roger Blench reached a similar conclusion from the vocabulary.

The honest version of this story is that both sides were reasoning carefully from real evidence. The disagreement was about which evidence counts most — and that question gets settled on the next slide.

Language lab

The disguise machine

Here is how the argument was settled. Owen Edwards showed in 2015 that Enggano's words are Austronesian — they have just been through an unusually brutal set of regular sound changes. Every m became b. Every n became d. Every t became k. Run a familiar Malay word through the machine and watch it turn into something unrecognisable.

Two more for free: telinga "ear" becomes e-kadiha, and the pronouns and numerals come straight from Proto-Malayo-Polynesian too. Pile up enough regular changes and a cousin stops looking like a cousin.

Language lab

One nasal, and the whole word changes

Enggano has a rule that runs across the entire word. If a word contains anything nasal, every vowel in it goes nasal, and every b turns into m and every d into n. If the word is oral, no nasal consonant can appear in it at all.

So m and b are not really different sounds in Enggano. They are the same sound wearing two different outfits, and the word decides which one. Linguists call this nasal harmony; Guaraní in South America does something similar.

  • That is why the word for "house," from an ancestor form *Rumaq, comes out as e-uba with a b — the word has no nasal vowel, so it cannot keep its m.

Counting

Twenty is "one person"

Enggano counts in twenties, and the reason is standing right in front of you: ten fingers and ten toes. kahaiʼ kak means "one person" and it means 20. A hundred is "five people."

  • Eight is kĩpãʼĩõp, from the verb "to hug" — both arms wrapped around something.
  • Nine is "eight, one coming." Seven is "five and two." Six looks like "three" said twice.
  • And 400 was recorded as "one our-body" — possibly two people counting together: every time one person finishes all twenty digits, the other counts off one of theirs. Twenty twenties.
  • Most speakers now use Malay numerals for anything large. One of the first parts of a language to go is usually the counting.

Cultural significance

Beehives, clans and mothers

  • Until about 1900, Enggano villages were clusters of round beehive houses on piles, roughly nine metres across, thatched with woven rattan leaves, entered by climbing a notched beam. Inside was one big room around a hearth. Each village had a kadiofe, an open-sided meeting hall.
  • Enggano society is matrilineal: you belong to your mother's clan. There are five original clans, with names drawn from trees and animals, each divided into smaller matrilineages.
  • There is a sixth clan — Kamay — and it is a modern invention: everyone who has settled on the island since is placed in it. A whole kinship system extended to absorb newcomers rather than exclude them.
  • In 1771 Charles Miller reported that women played a dominant role here. The Italian anthropologist Elio Modigliani spent three months on Enggano in 1891 and called his book L'Isola delle donne — "The Island of Women."

Modigliani's account is also the best record we have of pre-20th-century Enggano life — which means much of what is known about this culture was written by a visitor who stayed one season.

Why it matters now

Outnumbered at home

Enggano's problem is not the one most endangered languages have. The population is actually rising — it just isn't rising with Enggano speakers in it.

  • Indigenous Enggano are now roughly a third to a half of the island's residents, and in most villages the shared language is Indonesian or Bengkulu Malay, not Enggano.
  • School is in Indonesian. Government is in Indonesian. The ferry, the radio and the phone are in Indonesian. A child has almost no situation in which Enggano is the useful choice.
  • And the language keeps taking on Malay vocabulary — including, as we saw, the numbers. Researchers studying Enggano now spend real effort just separating which words are inherited and which arrived last century.

There is a modern twist. Indonesia's space agency has proposed Enggano as a spaceport site, citing its "low population and remoteness" — the same two facts that made it a place nobody protected.

What is being done

One German, one thesis, one team from Oxford

  • For most of the 20th century, nearly everything known about Enggano came from one researcher: Hans Kähler, who worked on the island in 1937 and spent fifty years publishing a grammar, text collections and a dictionary. His grammar and his dictionary disagree with each other, and parts of the handwriting cannot be read.
  • That is what a language with a single documenter looks like. Everything downstream — every classification argument — ran on those notebooks.
  • Things have changed fast. A 2011 thesis by Brendon Yoder re-described the vowels; Owen Edwards settled the family question in 2015; and since 2018 a team led by Mary Dalrymple at Oxford has worked with the five clans, recording stories and training younger speakers, including Engganese students.
  • In Indonesia, researchers at Gadjah Mada University have made a documentary about the language, and local students have been surveying the island's linguistic situation.

Note the difference between old and new documentation: Kähler took the language away in notebooks. The current projects train speakers from the island to do the work.

Why did it take so long?
Because the sound changes were so extreme that the resemblances were invisible. You can only see a regular correspondence if you line up enough words — and Enggano's documentation was thin, inconsistent, and written in German in the 1940s.

Is the puzzle fully solved?
No. Enggano is clearly Austronesian — but roughly 79% of its basic vocabulary still cannot be traced to Proto-Malayo-Polynesian. Did it come from an older, unknown language once spoken on the island, or did Enggano just replace its own words over time? Nobody knows.
click to reveal
Why care?
If there is a lost language underneath Enggano, its only surviving trace is in the vocabulary of 700 people. That would make Enggano the single remaining record of a people who left nothing else at all.
