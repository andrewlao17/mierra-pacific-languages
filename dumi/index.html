Dumi

दुमी · Khotang district, eastern Nepal

English has five forms of the verb "see." Dumi has hundreds — because the verb has to tell you who is doing it and who it is being done to, and how many of each there are

Where and who

The Kirat hills

  • Dumi is spoken around the Tap and Rava rivers in northern Khotang district, in the middle hills of eastern Nepal — terraced slopes of millet and maize, a long way below the high Himalaya and a long way above the plains.
  • Its speakers are Dumi Rai, one group among the Kirat peoples of eastern Nepal. The Rai alone speak something like two dozen different languages — not dialects, separate languages, often mutually unintelligible between neighbouring valleys.
  • The 2011 census recorded about 7,600 ethnic Dumi; the 2021 census about 8,600. How many speak Dumi is a much harder question, and it gets a slide of its own.
  • Three dialects are usually named — Kharbari, Lamdija and Makpa — with Makpa the most distinct, and the place where the language has held on best.

Dumi belongs to the Kiranti group, within the Sino-Tibetan family. Its distant relatives include Tibetan, Burmese and Mandarin — which tells you how much a family tree can hide.

Khotang district, Koshi Province, Nepal
Why so many languages in such a small area?

The middle hills are a landscape of steep ridges and deep river gorges. A village two valleys away might be a two-day walk. Communities stayed small, married locally and stayed put — and their speech drifted apart over centuries into genuinely separate languages.

You saw exactly the same pattern in Arunachal Pradesh and Papua New Guinea. Mountains and languages go together.

The context

A hundred and twenty languages, one school

  • Nepal has well over 120 languages from at least four different families. Nepali, an Indo-European language related to Hindi, is spoken by something like 17 million people and is the national language.
  • For much of the 20th century, state policy ran on the principle of one nation, one language. Nepali was the sole medium of education, the language of government, courts and advancement.
  • Every Dumi speaker is bilingual in Nepali. The reverse is not true, and has never needed to be.
  • Things changed on paper after 1990 and again under the 2015 constitution, which recognises the right to mother-tongue education. Turning that into actual classes, in a language with no textbooks and no trained teachers, is the hard part — and it is where Dumi is now.

Linguists who survey Nepal's Kiranti languages classify most as "threatened" and a handful, Dumi among them, as already "shifting" — the stage at which children are no longer learning it.

Two dozen Rai languages

Bantawa, Chamling, Kulung, Thulung, Khaling, Puma, Chintang, Wambule, Sampang, Yamphu, Lohorung, Mewahang, Dungmali, Nachhiring, Jero, Tilung, Koyee, Sam, Chhulung, Phangduwali, Dumi…

Each is a distinct language. Several are in worse shape than Dumi. A few have already gone. The whole group is, collectively, one of the great understudied regions of world linguistics — and one of the fastest-shrinking.

Language lab

The verb that knows everything

In English, "see" barely changes: see, sees, saw, seen, seeing. Five forms, and the verb tells you almost nothing about who is involved. You need separate words — I, you, them — to do that job.

Kiranti verbs do it differently. The verb agrees with both the subject and the object at once. Linguists call this biactantial agreement, and it means "I see you" and "you see me" use completely different verb forms, not just different pronouns.

Now multiply. Dumi distinguishes three persons and three numbers — singular, dual and plural — for the subject and the object. Use the grid, then read the counter.

George van Driem's 1993 grammar of Dumi devotes a substantial part of its length to the verb. His earlier paper on Dumi verbal morphology was a book-length study of the verb alone.

Language lab

Four different ways to say "we"

English has one word for "we," and it is permanently ambiguous. "We're going to the shop" — am I inviting you, or telling you I am leaving? Dumi, like other Kiranti languages, does not have that problem.

  • It distinguishes dual from plural: there is a form meaning "we two" and a different one meaning "we three or more."
  • And it distinguishes inclusive from exclusive: whether "we" includes the person you are talking to.
  • Combine those and you get four distinct words where English has one. Click them in the panel.

This is not decoration. In a village where everyone is related and who is included in a plan matters enormously, "we two, not you" and "we all, including you" are different social acts — and the grammar makes you choose.

You met clusivity before, in the Pazeh module in Taiwan. The dual is rarer still, and Kiranti languages carry both — and then build them into the verb as well as the pronouns.

Cultural significance

Mundhum and the dance

  • Kirat religious life runs on Mundhum — a vast body of oral scripture: origin stories, genealogies, ritual instructions and moral teaching, memorised and recited rather than written. Each Kirat group has its own.
  • Mundhum is recited by ritual specialists in a special register of the language: archaic vocabulary, paired phrases and formulas that nobody uses in ordinary speech. It is the deepest layer of the language and the hardest to document.
  • The great festival is Sakela (also Sakewa), danced twice a year — in spring when the planting begins and in autumn after the harvest — in a circle, following a leader, with movements drawn from the farming year and from birds and animals.
  • Ancestors matter continuously, not just at festivals. The household hearth, the lineage, the land and the dead are tied together, and the words for all of it are Dumi words.

Here is a detail that matters for the language's survival: Dumi speakers may use Nepali all day and still prefer to pray in Dumi. Ritual is often the last place a shifting language is used — which is both a lifeline and a warning sign.

Why does oral scripture matter here?
Because it cannot be separated from the language. A written scripture can be translated and keep working. A tradition held in memory, in a special register, performed aloud by trained reciters, dies with the last person who can perform it — and the ordinary vocabulary of the village is not enough to reconstruct it.

Is the culture also declining?
No — and this is now a familiar pattern in this course. Kirat identity has become stronger and more politically visible in Nepal over recent decades, Sakela is danced publicly in cities and in the diaspora, and young Rai people claim the heritage proudly. The culture is rising. The languages are not.

What about writing?
Dumi has been written mainly in Devanagari, the script used for Nepali. Some Kirat groups promote the Kirat Rai script (Sirijanga), tied to the identity movement. Script choice is never only technical: it is a statement about who you are and who you are not.

The number problem

Eight speakers, or two thousand five hundred?

Published figures for Dumi range from under 10 to 2,500. That is not a rounding error. It is a disagreement about what the question means.

The pattern you should take away: in a country where ethnic identity is politically significant, a census question about your mother tongue is partly a question about who you are. People answer honestly — about identity. That is not the same as a count of fluent speakers, and the two should never be quoted as if they were.

Think about the discipline

Who gets to write the grammar?

This module contains a shift that has happened across linguistics in one generation, and Dumi shows it unusually clearly.

In 1993, the first real grammar of Dumi was published by a Dutch linguist, George van Driem, working from roughly three months of fieldwork in a village in Khotang. It is a serious, careful book, and without it we would know far less.

Two decades later, grammars and dictionaries of Kiranti languages — Dumi among them — are increasingly being written by Rai linguists who grew up speaking them, with doctorates and university positions in Nepal.

Why it matters now

What a Kiranti verb is worth

  • For the Dumi: it is the language of Mundhum, of the ancestors, of the land in Khotang, and of a distinct identity inside a large and diverse state.
  • For linguistics: the Kiranti verb is one of the most complex agreement systems ever described, and the family is a crucial test case for how Sino-Tibetan languages work. Chinese, famously, has almost no verb agreement at all. Dumi has an enormous amount. Both are in the same family.
  • That contrast is a genuine scientific problem — and you cannot settle it without the small languages, because they are where the complicated end of the family lives.
  • What exists now: van Driem's grammar, newer grammars and dictionaries by Rai scholars, academic surveys, and community organisations working on teaching materials. What does not exist is a generation of children learning Dumi at home.

Nepal's constitution gives communities the right to mother-tongue education. Whether that becomes real for a language of a few thousand people, in a district with hard roads and tight budgets, is a political question, not a linguistic one.

The Sino-Tibetan puzzle

Mandarin Chinese: verbs do not change for person or number at all.

Dumi: verbs change for the person and number of both participants, plus tense, negation and more.

Same family. One of them started out that way and the other changed — and working out which is one of the live questions in the field. The answer is in the small Himalayan languages, and the clock on them is running.
