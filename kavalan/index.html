Rare Languages of the Asia-Pacific · Module 20

Kavalan

kbalan · 噶瑪蘭語 · eastern Taiwan

A language that lost its homeland, hid inside a neighbour's villages for a century, and came back with its own name

Watch the sea: your boat is sailing south, the way the Kavalan did.

Where and who

"The people of the plain"

  • The name says it: kbalan means people who live on the flat land — chosen to mark them off from the peoples of the mountains.
  • Their plain was the Lanyang Plain in Yilan, northeastern Taiwan, where they were the largest population for centuries. Qing officials wrote of "the 36 Kavalan villages," though there were more than sixty.
  • Today most Kavalan live far to the south, on the Hualien and Taitung coast. The population is roughly 1,500.
  • In 2000 researchers counted 24 speakers; a 2015 figure gives 70. Either way UNESCO calls it critically endangered, and nearly all speakers are elderly.

Kavalan belongs to the East Formosan branch of Austronesian — a distant cousin of Malay, Malagasy and Māori.

Phonetics lab

A sound almost no language has

Kavalan has a consonant written ɮ. Fewer than a handful of the world's languages use it. It is an l and a z happening at the same time: your tongue blocks the middle of your mouth like an l, and the air hisses out around the sides like a z.

Use the lab to build it up one feature at a time, then try it yourself — tongue tip on the ridge behind your teeth, push air out of the sides, and turn your voice on.

Some Kavalan speakers pronounce it closer to a plain d. Kavalan also packs in consonant clusters and doubled consonants that are rare in Taiwan's other languages.

The journey

A people who kept having to sail

Kavalan history is a chain of departures. Their own origin story begins with a sea crossing; their modern history is another one, forced. They were known as skilled sailors and traders — a useful thing to be when you keep losing the land.

  • Oral tradition says the ancestors came from islands to the south, stopping at a place called Sanasai before reaching the Lanyang Plain.
  • In the 1800s, as settlers took the plain, families loaded boats and sailed south down the coast to the Hualien plain and beyond.
  • The villages they founded carry the names of the villages they left — a map of the old homeland, redrawn 100 km away.

One syllable, one whole idea

English builds meanings out of extra words: "pretend to," "smell of," "speak the language of." Kavalan can do those jobs with a single prefix glued to the front of a word. Linguists have catalogued dozens of them, and some are startlingly specific. Step through a few.

Cultural significance

Cloth made from banana trees

  • Banana-fibre weaving is unique to the Kavalan among Taiwan's indigenous peoples. The cloth is cool and light — made for a hot coast — and was used for clothing, bags and rice sacks.
  • When the recognition campaign began in the 1980s, the community needed something only they did, to prove they still existed. The weaving became evidence.
  • There was one problem: nobody was weaving any more. Yu-En Yen and others tracked down three elders who remembered watching their grandmothers do it, and rebuilt the craft from those memories. A workshop opened in Xinshe in 2005.
  • In 2021 Taiwan's Ministry of Culture named Yu-En Yen a preserver of an important traditional craft: ni tenunan tu benina, Kavalan banana-fibre weaving.

Before cutting a banana trunk she performs paspaw, an offering asking the ancestors' blessing. The craft and the language carry the same vocabulary — every tool and step has a Kavalan name.

Who holds the knowledge

The women who call the ancestors

  • Kavalan society is matrilineal, and every shaman — metiyu — is a woman. Leaders, by contrast, are elected.
  • Japanese-era bans and new religions ended most of the old ceremonies. One survived almost everywhere: palilin, the family ancestor ritual held at the turn of the year.
  • That survival matters more than it sounds. Ritual words are among the last places a language lives once daily conversation has switched — and ritual protocol is now used to tell which families descend from which old village.
  • A traditional marker of the new year: the tiger's claw tree coming into flower.

Think it through

Two cousins, one people, two answers

In 2002 the Kavalan won recognition — but not all of them got it. Those in Hualien, who had kept the language and ceremonies alive in their own villages, were registered. Most Kavalan descendants still living in Yilan, the original homeland, were not: Japanese officials had already recorded them as assimilated, and the paperwork followed them for a century.

When Kavalan from Hualien visited Yilan in recent decades, some were astonished to find relatives there at all. Their elders had told them everyone had left.

In 2019, Kavalan in Yilan held their first sea and harvest festivals in over a hundred years, helped by their Hualien cousins. The slogan they chose: "We have always been here."

Why it matters now

Not just rare — irreplaceable

  • A 2017 study borrowed a method from conservation biology, which ranks animals by how endangered they are and how unlike anything else they are. Applied to languages, Kavalan came out as one of the most lexically distinctive languages in the whole Austronesian family.
  • In plain terms: Kavalan has a lot of words that resemble nothing else. Lose it, and you don't lose a copy of something — you lose the only one.
  • There is real machinery behind the revival: a published dictionary and reference grammar, government-produced teaching materials, an online dictionary, school mother-tongue classes, and a village workshop where the weaving vocabulary is used daily.
  • The obstacle is arithmetic. Fluent speakers are elderly, and the people learning Kavalan mostly learn it as a second language, in classrooms, surrounded by Mandarin.
