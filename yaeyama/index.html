Yaeyama

Yaimamuni · ヤイマムニ

Nineteen islands, one language family, and a pattern of five and four that means forever

Watch the right edge: a minsā belt weaves itself a little further with every slide.

Where and who

Japan's southernmost islands

  • The Yaeyama Islands sit southwest of Okinawa and east of Taiwan. Together they hold about 53,000 people. Speakers call the language Yaimamuni, "Yaeyama speech."
  • It is a Southern Ryukyuan language, in the same family as Japanese but not understandable to Japanese speakers. Japan's government still files it as a "dialect."
  • Roughly 7,000–10,000 people can speak it, and most are over 50. UNESCO lists it as definitely endangered.
  • Here is the twist: each island has its own variety, and some of them cannot understand each other either.

Nearby Yonaguni speech is counted as a separate language, not part of Yaeyama.

Pronunciation tip

Long vowels matter. Ōritōri holds the ō twice as long as a short o — shorten them and the word stops sounding like Yaeyama.

Grammar you can feel

The word that points

Yaeyama puts its words in the same order as Japanese: the verb comes last. But it does one thing English and Japanese don't.

  • When you ask who, what, where, the question word has to carry a little spotlight: du.
  • Leave the du off and the sentence isn't just odd — speakers say it's simply not Yaeyama.
  • English has nothing like it. The closest feeling is the stress in "WHO ate it?" — except in Yaeyama it's a real word you must say.

Linguists call du a focus marker. Yaeyama is one of the languages that keeps a focus system Japanese mostly lost.

Cloth that talks

Five and four: minsā

  • Minsā is a cotton belt woven on Taketomi and Ishigaki, indigo blue with white squares. Its pattern repeats five squares, then four.
  • That's a pun. In Japanese, five is itsu — which also means "when" — and four is yo, which also means "age" or "world." Five and four reads as itsu no yo made mo: "forever."
  • A young woman wove one and gave it to the man she loved. The narrow zigzag edging looks like centipede legs, and carried a second message: come and visit often.
  • Yaeyama minsā became a nationally recognised traditional craft in 1989, and is still woven on upright looms today.

A whole sentence, said with no letters at all — and readable by anyone, in any language.

Songs and spirits

Yunta, and the visitors from gusō

  • Yunta are work songs: one person calls a line, everyone else answers a chorus, and the rhythm keeps the whole field planting together. No instrument needed — just voices.
  • The most famous is Asadoya Yunta from Taketomi, about Asato Kuyama (1722–1799) and a government official who proposed to her. On Okinawa people sing the version where she refuses him; on Taketomi they sing the one where she doesn't.
  • In 1934 a record company put new Japanese lyrics on the tune and it became a nationwide hit — so most of Japan knows the melody without knowing a word of the Yaeyama original.
  • At Obon, masked ancestors come back. Ushumai (grandfather) and Nmi (grandmother) visit houses with dancers and answer the crowd's questions about the afterlife, gusō — in Yaeyama, in high squeaky voices, and always as a joke.

Three island treasures

Yaeyama is the only place on earth for a lot of things — a cat, a kind of sand, and a view of the sky that the rest of Japan never gets. Explore all three in the panel.

Iriomote Island is more than ninety percent jungle and mangrove. In 2021 it was added, with other southern islands, to the UNESCO World Heritage list for its wildlife.

Words carry habitats too: when a language goes quiet, the local names for plants, fish, reefs and weather usually go with it.

Hoshizuna, "star sand," washes up on Taketomi's beaches. Each grain is the tiny shell of a single-celled sea creature.

The Iriomote cat was first identified in 1965 and lives in the smallest range of any wild cat on earth. It's listed as critically endangered; islanders run road-kill patrols and keep stray cats off the island.

Hateruma is the southernmost inhabited island in Japan, and the only place in the country where the Southern Cross clears the horizon well enough to see.

Why it matters now

Spoken in songs, barely in kitchens

  • Researchers report that people under 60 mostly use Yaeyama only in songs and rituals. Children grow up speaking Japanese.
  • For a century, school and government called it a dialect. A thing called a dialect is easy to treat as something to grow out of.
  • Yaeyama is written down only rarely, so when a speaker dies, a whole way of saying things can go with them.
Dictionaries
Scholars born in the islands began recording Yaeyama vocabulary in the early 1900s, and word collections keep being published today.
