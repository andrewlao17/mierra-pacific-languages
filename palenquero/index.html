Palenquero

Lengua ri Palenge · San Basilio de Palenque, Colombia

Almost every word comes from Spanish. Almost no Spanish speaker can understand a sentence of it. And the town that speaks it bought its freedom from the Spanish Crown in 1691

Watch the background: the palisade that gave the place its name is going up.

Where and who

A town built behind a fence

  • A palenque was a fortified village built by people who escaped slavery. Dozens existed across the Spanish Americas. San Basilio de Palenque, about 60 km inland from Cartagena, is the one that survived.
  • Around 1599, a group led by Benkos Biohó escaped into the forest and founded a settlement. Biohó declared himself king, the palenque drew more escapees, and the Spanish could not dislodge it.
  • In 1691 the Spanish Crown issued a royal decree granting the people of Palenque their freedom — on the condition that they stop taking in new escapees. Freedom, bought with a hard bargain.
  • The town has a few thousand people. Estimates of Palenquero put roughly 2,000–3,000 with full command and more with passive understanding. There are no monolingual speakers left — everyone also speaks Spanish.

Speakers do not usually call it Palenquero. They call it Lengua — "the language" — or lengua ri Palenge.

San Basilio de Palenque, Bolívar, Colombia
Why Cartagena matters

Cartagena was one of the two main ports of the Spanish slave trade in the Americas. Hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans passed through it — and the people who escaped into the hills behind it brought their languages with them.

Research over the past forty years points to one African language above all in Palenque's background: Kikongo, from west-central Africa. Possibly the only significant one.

Clear the ground

What a creole actually is

"Creole" is one of the most misused words in popular writing about language. It does not mean broken, simplified, or a dialect of anything. Step through the panel.

The short version: when people with no shared language are thrown together — which is exactly what the slave trade did, deliberately — they build a pidgin. When children grow up with that pidgin, they turn it into a full language with full grammar. That is a creole.

Palenquero takes nearly all its vocabulary from Spanish, and nearly none of its grammar. That is why a Spanish speaker recognises the words and cannot follow the sentence.

Compare the Michif module. Michif was made by people fluent in both parents. A creole is made by children out of material that was not a full language when they got it. Different process, different result — and both are complete human languages.

The words will barely change. The grammar changes completely. Start from a Spanish sentence and apply the rules of Lengua, one at a time.

Negation at the very end of the sentence is the single most striking feature of Palenquero, and it is a pattern found in parts of west-central Africa — not in Spanish.

Where the words come from

Three sources in one vocabulary

The everyday vocabulary of Palenquero is overwhelmingly Spanish, reshaped by sound changes — posada became posá, mujer became mujé, perro became pelo.

Around 300 words of African origin have been identified, most traced to Kikongo. And a handful look Portuguese — a trace of the Portuguese-run slave trade that brought people to Cartagena.

Sort the words in the panel. Some will surprise you.

Note which words stayed African: the plural marker, two pronouns, words for cattle and for child. Those are not casual loans. A people's own pronouns are among the last things to be replaced.

Why it nearly died

"Don't speak like that"

  • Through most of the 20th century, Palenqueros who left the village for Cartagena were mocked for how they spoke. The language was treated as evidence of being rural, Black and uneducated — three things Colombian society punished.
  • So parents did the rational thing: they stopped passing it on. By the mid-1980s, linguists visiting Palenque were convinced the language was finished. One who first came in 1985 has described attitudes toward anything Black as so negative that change seemed impossible.
  • That is the standard mechanism, and you have now seen it in a dozen modules: a language dies because speaking it costs you something, and parents love their children.
  • What makes Palenquero unusual is what happened next.

By the time the revival began, there were already no monolingual Palenquero speakers left. Every speaker was and is bilingual, and switches between Lengua and Spanish depending on who is present.

Why was it stigmatised?
Because it marked you as Palenquero, and being Palenquero meant being descended from enslaved Africans in a society organised around that fact. The language was never the real target. It was just the most audible sign.

What changed the attitude?
Three things, researchers suggest. A boxer — Antonio Cervantes, "Kid Pambelé", from Palenque, who became a world champion in the 1970s and made the town famous. An ethnoeducation movement from the late 1970s onward. And UNESCO's 2005 declaration, which turned the language from an embarrassment into an internationally recognised treasure.

Did it work?
Remarkably, yes — in attitude. Palenquero is now a source of pride, taught in the local school from preschool upward, and used publicly. In actual fluent use among young people, the picture is more complicated, which is slide ten.

Cultural significance

Drums, age-sets and nine nights

  • The great ceremony is the lumbalú, the funeral rite. The community is called together by the pechiche drum, and there follow nine days and nights of songs, dances and laments for the dead. Some of its chants preserve African language fragments that are not used anywhere else in daily speech.
  • Palenque society is organised partly by kuagro: groups of people of the same age who grow up together and keep mutual obligations for life. Your kuagro is a second family — and the word appears in everyday Palenquero sentences.
  • The village is also famous for its music — the Sexteto Tabalá and the champeta tradition — and for the palenqueras, women who sell fruit from bowls balanced on their heads and have become one of Colombia's best-known images.
  • In 2005 UNESCO proclaimed the Cultural Space of Palenque de San Basilio a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. The language sits at the centre of that designation.

Note how the pieces interlock: the funeral rite carries the oldest language, the age-sets carry the social structure, and the music carries both out into the world.

The extraordinary part

A creole that came back

This is genuinely rare. Creole languages almost never get revived — they are usually the low-prestige partner in a two-language society, and the pressure runs one way only.

  • From 1992, the public school in Palenque began teaching Palenquero, as part of Colombia's etnoeducación framework. It is now taught from preschool through secondary school.
  • Community linguists from Palenque itself have compiled lexicons and teaching materials. The UNESCO designation in 2005 brought attention, funding and — crucially — status.
  • One linguist who had predicted the language's death has written about being wrong. Young people in Palenque now learn Lengua at school and claim it as theirs.

But a revival is not a restoration. What the school produces is not the same thing the elders speak — and the next slide is about that gap, which is the most interesting problem in this module.

Who speaks what, now

Elders: the most fluent speakers, who acquired Lengua as a first language. Many had little formal schooling in Spanish.

Middle-aged adults: raised away from the language in childhood — and then the first cohort through the revival programme in the 1990s.

Young people: learning it at school as a second language, with pride, but not yet using it with each other outside class.

Think it through

The ma problem

In Palenquero, ma goes in front of a noun to mark the plural: ma posá, "houses." It comes from Kikongo, and it is the single most recognisably African piece of grammar in the language.

Researchers studying young learners found something unexpected. Students who learn Palenquero at school use ma constantly — including in front of singular nouns, where fluent elders never use it.

For them, ma has stopped being a plural marker. It has become a badge: the thing you put in a sentence to show that what you are speaking is Lengua and not Spanish.

So — is that a mistake to be corrected, or a language changing? Take a position in the panel before you read the arguments.

One explanation researchers offer: the classes focus on vocabulary rather than grammar, with little correction and little conversation between learners and fluent elders. Learners are, in a sense, shadow-boxing.
