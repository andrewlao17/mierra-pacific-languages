Kanakanavu

A language of the river valleys of southern Taiwan

Our first word: mikong, "millet."

Who and where

People of the Namasia valley

  • The Kanakanavu live in Manga and Takanua villages in Namasia District, Kaohsiung, along the Nanzixian River.
  • They number fewer than 500 people, a minority even among Taiwan's Indigenous peoples.
  • For decades they were officially counted as Tsou. Then, at a 2001 language test, Kanakanavu and Tsou speakers found they couldn't understand each other.
  • That sparked a campaign. On 26 June 2014, Taiwan recognized the Kanakanavu as its 16th Indigenous people, on the same day as their neighbours, the Hla'alua.
Namasia, Kaohsiung, Taiwan

How endangered?

A handful of voices

fluent speakers (about 6 in 2014)Kanakanavu people (about 360 in 2020)
  • UNESCO lists Kanakanavu as critically endangered. In 2014, its few fluent speakers were all over 60.
  • Like Hla'alua, it has three sets of numbers, for counting aloud, counting things and counting people.

Mikong

The millet ceremony

  • Every autumn, after the millet harvest, the Kanakanavu thank the gods and their ancestors.
  • Elders ask: "Who had a good dream last night?" The best dreamer lights the fire at the cakuru to begin.
  • Four offerings go on the altar: rice cake, meat, wine and fish.
  • People press small pieces of sticky rice cake onto the house's posts, praying the community will stick together and its buildings stand strong.

Kaisisi Cakʉran

The river ceremony

The legend: long ago, a young man named Namasia found the river muddy. Going upstream, he discovered a giant eel blocking the water. A wild boar came forward to help kill it, and the river flowed again. The river was named Namasia after him, and the district still carries his name.

The ceremony: before dawn, the men go to the river, and the officiant reports to the river god in the Kanakanavu language. Pork, peppers and sour foods are forbidden, because they could pollute the river.

Why we only describe it: the river ceremony is attended only by Kanakanavu men. In 2021 the community applied to have it recognized as official Indigenous intangible cultural heritage.

Hope

Rebuilding a language

  • In 2009, Typhoon Morakot devastated Namasia. Families rebuilt their villages, and their culture, from the ground up.
  • The community runs classes in songs, dances and the language, plus apprenticeships in traditional planting.
  • A documentation project has been recording the language since 2012, and an online Kanakanavu dictionary is free for anyone to use.
  • A Young Kanakanavu Association brings young people together to learn from elders and share their culture.
