Yaghnobi

Яғнобӣ зивок · yaɣnobī́ zivók

A mountain language connected to the traders of the Silk Road

Follow the silk thread on the left through 1,300 years.

Who and where

From the "ice valley"

  • Yaghnobi comes from the Yaghnob Valley in northwestern Tajikistan. Scholars link the name to "ice valley" or "ice river."
  • Most speakers now live in places such as Zafarobod, Varzob and Dushanbe, far from the valley.
  • Yaghnobi is the home language in many families. Tajik is often used in school, business and official life.
  • UNESCO lists it as definitely endangered.

Yaghnob Valley

Silk Road story

A shepherd finds a basket

300s

Sogdian merchants send letters along trade routes between Central Asia and China.

722

Dewashtich, ruler of Panjakent, is defeated at Mount Mugh during the Arab conquest.

1932

A shepherd finds a basket holding a manuscript on Mount Mugh. Sogdian documents soon follow.

The link: Sogdian, once a major language of the Silk Road, died out. Yaghnobi survived. It likely comes from a spoken Sogdian dialect, not from the written Sogdian in those documents.

𐼼𐼴𐼶𐼹𐼷𐼸 The word for "Sogdian" in one Sogdian script

Legend or history? Some Yaghnobis trace their history to people fleeing the Arab conquest. One scholar says clear evidence is limited, but the story remains important to the community.

One valley, two ways to say it

Western and eastern Yaghnobi changed old Sogdian sounds in different ways. Switch sides of the valley:

The old θ sounds like th in "think." Transitional dialects lie between west and east.

Speak along

Sounds from the mountains

Two sounds matter here. ɣ is voiced and made far back in the mouth. x is like the end of Scottish "loch."

  1. Pretend to clear your throat very gently. That's close to x.
  2. Turn on your voice while doing it. That moves you toward ɣ.
  3. Say ɣar, "mountain," softly and slowly.
  4. Finish with ēx, "ice," then yaɣnobī́.

Count to ten

Yaghnobi kept its own numbers to ten. For larger numbers, people often use Tajik numbers.

Sources also record older forms, such as dū bīst, "two twenties," for 40.

Forced move

From snow to cotton

1970

Soviet authorities force roughly 3,000 to 4,000 people from the valley, many by helicopter, to cotton lands in Zafarobod.

1970s

Heat, hard labor and shock take many lives. Some families secretly return.

1978

Some returnees are forced out again.

1980s–90s

Families gain the right to return. Only some do.

A dark pun: people sometimes turned Zafarobod into Zahfarōbṓd, using zah, a word for "mud" or "filth."

Today

How do you keep a language alive after moving?

Yaghnob is beautiful but remote. Snow can block roads for six months or more, and children may leave for boarding school where they study in Tajik. Villager Safarali Boqiev told reporters that he stayed because the language could disappear if everyone left.
