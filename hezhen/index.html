Hezhen / Nanai

赫哲 · нанай · the Amur River, China and Russia

One people, one language, and a river that became an international border in 1858 — so that half of them are now a Russian minority and half a Chinese one, with two names, two scripts and two completely different fates

Watch the water. The two banks are starting to look different.

Where and who

People of the river

  • They live along the Amur, the Ussuri and the Sungari — the great rivers of the Russian Far East and north-east China. Fishers above all, and hunters.
  • The language is Southern Tungusic: the same branch you met in the Uilta module, and a close relative of Uilta and Ulch. Its distant cousin is historical Manchu.
  • Their self-name is Nani — roughly "people of this place." The Chinese name Hezhen is explained as "people who live along the lower course of the river." Russians long called them Gold or Goldi.
  • In Russia the 2010 census counted 12,003 Nanai; in China the Hezhe numbered 5,373 in 2020 — the second-smallest of China's 56 recognised ethnic groups.

Roughly 18,000 people in total, one language, and a political line through the middle of their homeland. This module is about what that line did.

The Amur basin — Khabarovsk Krai, Russia and Heilongjiang, China
The river is the point

For this culture the Amur is not a boundary, it is the centre. Fish supplied food, trade, tools, thread and clothing; the calendar ran on salmon runs; settlements faced the water.

Which is exactly why the 19th-century treaties were so destructive. They turned the middle of a world into the edge of two.

The experiment

The same language, run twice

This is as close as endangered-language studies gets to a controlled experiment. One language, one people, split by a border in 1858, then subjected to two very different states for 170 years.

Cultural significance

Clothes made of fish

  • The Hezhen are the only people in China — and among very few anywhere — to have developed a complete clothing tradition from fish skin: coats, trousers, boots, gloves, thread and all.
  • It is a brilliant solution to a specific problem. On the Amur you have abundant large fish and limited large game, and fish skin, properly prepared, is light, tough and water-resistant — which matters enormously if you spend your life in a boat.
  • The practice gave them an exonym: Chinese sources called them Yupi Dazi, the "fish-skin Tartars." They were also known as the dog-using people, for the sled dogs they hunted with.

Every stage of that process has its own vocabulary — the species, the tools, the state of the skin at each point. When the craft becomes a heritage demonstration performed in Mandarin, those words have nothing left to do.

The epic

Yimakan

  • The Hezhen had no script of their own. Everything — history, genealogy, mythology, law, survival knowledge — was carried in speech. The grandest form of that is Yimakan: long narrative epics, partly sung and partly spoken, performed solo, without instruments.
  • A Yimakan performance can run for days. The stories tell of tribal alliances, battles, heroes and shamanic journeys, and they are performed at weddings, funerals, hunts and the arrival of guests.
  • Yimakan sits alongside other genres — telungu legends, shuohuli tales, jialinkuo songs. Click them in the panel.
  • And here is the point for this course: Yimakan is performed in Hezhen. You cannot teach the epic without teaching the language. The two cannot be separated, and that turns out to matter enormously.

Compare the Uilta module, where the culture was documented but the language went anyway; and Kristang, where the festivals thrive while the creole fades. A tradition that requires the language is a different kind of asset.

The twentieth century

Three hundred people

  • The treaties of 1858 and 1860 fixed the Amur as the Russia–China border. A people who had moved freely along both banks woke up as subjects of two empires, with their relatives on the far shore.
  • Then came the twentieth century. During the Japanese occupation of Manchuria, the Hezhen on the Chinese side were displaced, forced into resettlement and devastated by disease and opium. Chinese accounts put their number at only about 300 people by the end of the Second World War.
  • From roughly 300 in 1945 to over 5,000 today is a real demographic recovery. The language did not recover with it.
  • That gap — population up, language down — is the single most repeated pattern in this entire course. You saw it with the Gelao, the Métis, the Kristang and the Rapa Nui.

On the Russian side the pressures were different in kind and similar in effect: collectivisation, resettlement into consolidated villages, Russian-language boarding schools.

Where the language stands

Eleven per cent, and falling

  • In Russia: the 2010 census recorded about 1,347 Nanai speakers — roughly 11% of the ethnic population — and the 2021 figure is around a thousand. UNESCO classes Nanai as severely endangered.
  • The language has held on best in the Nanai District of Khabarovsk Krai, which has ethnic autonomous status, an active community, published books and school textbooks. Even there, estimates of native speakers run to only about 100–150.
  • In China: figures vary from roughly a hundred fluent speakers down to a handful of elders; UNESCO's Red Book has listed Hezhe as nearly extinct. Everyone is bilingual in Mandarin, and Chinese characters are used for writing.
  • The split also shaped the writing. Russia gave Nanai a Latin alphabet in the 1930s and then Cyrillic; China never gave Hezhen a script at all.

Note the dialect geography: the variety spoken in China is called Hezhe or Qile'en; the Russian side has Najkhin, Kur-Urmi, Bikin and others. A border does not just divide speakers — over time it divides a language.

Does autonomy help?
It appears to. The one place Nanai has best survived is the district with ethnic autonomous status, an active community of writers and teachers, and books in the language. That is three modules' worth of evidence in one data point — but even there, native speakers number in the low hundreds. Autonomy slows the clock; it has not stopped it.

Does a script matter?
The Russian side got an alphabet in the 1930s and used it to publish textbooks. The Chinese side got none and writes in Chinese characters. The Russian side has more speakers. That is suggestive, not proof — the populations and policies differ in many other ways too — but it echoes the Uilta module exactly.

Can the two halves help each other?
They have started to. Russian Nanai have been invited to Hezhen festivals in China, and reports describe older Hezhen men and visiting Nanai speaking their own varieties and understanding one another across a border their great-grandparents did not have. Rare good news

A thing that almost never happens

  • In 2011, UNESCO inscribed Hezhen Yimakan storytelling on the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding — the emergency ward. At that point the tradition had come down to a very small number of practising storytellers.
  • What followed was a sustained safeguarding programme: transmission classes, recording of surviving performers, support for apprentices, and teaching in schools. In Tongjiang, a primary school teaches Yimakan alongside fish-skin craft and pottery.
  • In December 2025, UNESCO moved Yimakan off the urgent list and onto the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity — and added the safeguarding programme to its Register of Good Safeguarding Practices.
  • Things essentially never get better in this field. This one did.

And UNESCO's own account credits the school teaching with two outcomes: it got young people interested — and it helped keep the Hezhen language alive, because you cannot perform the epic in Mandarin.

Read it carefully, though

What was taken off the urgent list is the storytelling tradition, not the language. Hezhen still has very few fluent speakers, and performing an epic is not the same as chatting at home.

What it does prove is something this course has argued all the way through: a language survives when it has a job to do. Give people a reason to use it that cannot be done in the big language, and the arithmetic changes.

Nani

"People of this place." Thank you — that's the course. Questions?

At a festival on the Chinese bank of the Amur, old Hezhen men spoke Hezhen and visiting Russian Nanai spoke Nanai, and they understood each other perfectly — across a border drawn in 1858 by two governments that never asked them.

That is what a language is: not a flag, not a border, not a census line. A way for people to recognise each other. Every module in this course has been about somebody trying to keep one.
