Ainu

The Indigenous language of Hokkaido, Japan

Our first Ainu word: irankarapte, a greeting.

Who speaks Ainu?

  • The Ainu are the Indigenous people of Hokkaido, Japan's northern island. Ainu was also once spoken on Sakhalin, the Kuril Islands and in northern Honshu.
  • In Ainu, the word aynu means "human".
  • Ainu is a language isolate: it has no proven relatives. It is not related to Japanese.
  • Today it is endangered, with very few native speakers left.

Ainu elder Kayano Shigeru explained irankarapte as "let me gently touch your heart." Scholars don't all agree on this meaning, and it was traditionally a formal greeting, so use it respectfully.

Stories that are sung

  • Yukar are long sung epics about human heroes.
  • Kamuy yukar are "songs of the gods." In them, a kamuy (a god or spirit, often an animal) tells its own story in the first person. They were usually sung by women.
  • The owl is kotan kor kamuy, the god who guards the village.

Ainu was traditionally passed on by speaking and singing, not by writing. These songs kept history, values and knowledge of nature alive for generations.

Speak along: the owl god's song

In 1922, a 19-year-old Ainu woman named Chiri Yukie wrote down 13 songs of the gods in Ainu and translated them into Japanese. She died the day she finished; the book came out in 1923 and was the first collection of its kind written by an Ainu person.

Why is Ainu endangered?

  • For more than a century, the Ainu faced forced assimilation and discrimination. An 1899 law pushed Ainu people to give up their way of life, and Japanese replaced Ainu in daily use.
  • 1994: Kayano Shigeru became the first Ainu member of Japan's parliament, and asked questions there in Ainu.
  • 1997: the 1899 law was finally repealed, and a new law began supporting Ainu language and culture.
  • 2019: a new law recognized the Ainu as an Indigenous people for the first time.

Ainu today: bringing it back

  • Upopoy, the National Ainu Museum and Park, opened in Shiraoi on 12 July 2020. Its name means "singing together in a large group."
  • Since 2013, the Irankarapte campaign has encouraged people across Hokkaido to use the Ainu greeting.
  • Ainu is taught in community classes and through a radio course with free lesson books.
  • Young Ainu people are learning songs, dances and crafts from their elders.
