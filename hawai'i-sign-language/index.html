Hawai'i Sign Language

HSL · Hawaiʻi, United States

A language isolate, used in one of the most studied places on earth, that linguistics did not formally recognise until 2013 — and that fewer than ten people now sign fluently

First, clear the ground

Four things people get wrong

This module will not make sense unless we start here. Most people — including most people who have met a sign language — carry a set of assumptions about them that are simply false, and the falsehoods are the reason HSL went unrecognised for a century.

Sign languages are full human languages with their own grammar, their own history and their own family trees. They can be related to each other, borrow from each other, split into dialects and become endangered — exactly like spoken languages, because they are languages.

Where and who

A language that grew up on the islands

  • Deaf people have always been born everywhere, including Hawaiʻi. Where enough of them come together, a sign language develops — and in Hawaiʻi one did, probably by the late 1800s.
  • It is a language isolate: it grew up in the islands, not imported, and it is not descended from any other known sign language.
  • In 2013, researchers estimated around 40 people still used HSL, nearly all over 80. By 2021, reporting put the number of fluent signers in single figures.
  • A further group of about forty people — the generation in between — use CHSL, a blend of HSL and ASL that formed as the two languages collided. We will come back to them.

Note how this changes what "indigenous language of Hawaiʻi" means. Hawaiian — ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi — is one. HSL is another, with a completely separate history, and it is in far worse shape.

Hawaiʻi, United States
How does a sign language start?

Put Deaf children together — in a school, a village, a neighbourhood — and within a generation or two they will build a full language with grammar, whether or not any adult teaches them one. It has been observed happening in real time, most famously in Nicaragua in the 1980s.

This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence we have that humans are built for language, not merely for speech.

How it was recognised

1 March 2013

  • The person at the centre is Linda Yuen Lambrecht. Born Deaf, she began picking up HSL at about six months old from her older Deaf brothers, and grew up to teach American Sign Language at Kapiʻolani Community College.
  • She had spent years saying that the signing of the kūpuna — the elders — was not ASL and not a dialect of ASL. In 2010 she approached linguists at the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa.
  • A team formed: Lambrecht with Barbara Earth, James "Woody" Woodward and William O'Grady, and later others. They located and interviewed elderly signers across the islands and compared the vocabulary systematically.
  • On 1 March 2013, at a conference on language documentation held in Honolulu, they announced that HSL is a distinct language — described at the time as possibly the last undiscovered language in the United States.

"Discovered" is the wrong word, and the same problem came up in the Koro module. Deaf people in Hawaiʻi had been using HSL for a century. What changed in 2013 is that it entered the written record — which is what determines funding, recognition and whether anybody tries to save it.

Why did it take so long?
Because everyone assumed. Hearing people assumed all signing was ASL. Linguists studying Hawaiʻi studied spoken languages. And Deaf signers who used HSL had been told for decades that what they were doing was bad ASL, not a different language — so they often did not claim otherwise.

Who did the work?
A Deaf community member led it. Lambrecht's own knowledge, her standing in the community and her ability to sign with elders are the reason the documentation exists. The hearing linguists brought comparative method and grant applications. Neither half would have worked alone.

Was everyone pleased?
No. There has been real disagreement inside Hawaiʻi's Deaf community about whether HSL is a separate language and whether it is worth preserving — partly because of the decades of stigma attached to it. Communities are not single-minded, and a revival that pretends otherwise will fail.

Think like a linguist

Different language, or just different style?

The claim "this is a separate language" needs evidence, and the evidence here is the same kind used in the Koro module: take a standard list of basic vocabulary — everyday, culture-independent concepts — and count how many items match.

For sign languages the researchers compare the building blocks of each sign. If two varieties share 80% or more of basic vocabulary, they are usually treated as dialects of one language. Below that, separate languages.

Language lab

What a sign is made of

A spoken word is built from sounds. Change one sound in bat and you get pat — a different word. Sign languages work the same way, with different raw material.

Every sign is built from a small number of parameters: the shape of the hand, where it is placed, how it moves, which way the palm faces, and what the face and body are doing. Change one and you can change the word completely.

Build a sign in the panel and watch the parameters change.

The face is grammar, not decoration. In many sign languages, raised eyebrows turn a statement into a question, and a particular mouth shape marks something as done carelessly. A signer with a blank face is speaking in monotone.

The surprise

The thing HSL doesn't have

Here is why HSL matters to linguistics far beyond Hawaiʻi.

  • Most documented sign languages use verbal classifiers: handshapes that stand for a whole category of thing — a vehicle, a flat object, a standing person — and then move through space to show what that thing did. ASL uses them constantly.
  • They were widely assumed to be a universal of sign languages: a feature so natural to the visual medium that every sign language would develop it.
  • HSL does not have them.

That single fact rearranges a theoretical claim. If one sign language can do without a feature thought to be inevitable, then that feature is a choice languages make, not a consequence of using the hands. You cannot learn that from the big, well-studied sign languages. You learn it from the small ones.

This is the argument of the whole course, stated in a single example: the languages with the fewest speakers are often the ones carrying the most information about what human language can be.

Why "universals" need small languages

Suppose you study the ten largest sign languages and find a feature in all ten. It looks universal. But those ten are mostly related to each other, or have been in contact, or were shaped by the same deaf-education movements.

A genuinely isolated language — one that grew up on its own, as HSL did — is the only real test. Which means every isolate that disappears takes a test with it.

Discuss: if a rule holds in 99 languages and fails in one with eight speakers, is it still a rule? What should a scientist do with the exception?

History

The school that banned signing and saved the language

  • In 1914 a school for Deaf children opened in Honolulu. Its philosophy was oralist: students were to learn to speak and lip-read, and signing was discouraged or forbidden. That was the mainstream of deaf education worldwide at the time.
  • And yet the school is the main reason HSL survived into the 20th century — because it gathered Deaf children in one place. They signed to each other out of sight of the teachers, and passed the language to each new intake of children.
  • Then came the reversal. From around the 1940s, visitors and teachers from the US mainland brought ASL, and told Hawaiʻi's Deaf community that their signing was primitive and that ASL was better.
  • Lambrecht has described elders becoming embarrassed and switching to ASL because they had absorbed the idea that HSL was not good enough. That is how a language dies without anybody passing a law.

Hold both halves of this together: the institution that tried to suppress signing accidentally preserved a language, and the arrival of a related kind of liberation — a prestigious, fully recognised sign language — is what finally killed it.

The same pattern, three modules

Kavalan: hid inside Amis villages to survive, and nearly lost the language doing it.

Gelao: official recognition of the people, no support for the language.

HSL: saved by a school that banned it, killed by a language that freed its signers.

None of these are stories with a villain. They are stories about incentives — which is exactly why they keep repeating.

Language contact, live

The generation in the middle

When two languages meet in one community, the result is rarely a clean switch. It is usually a layered set of generations, and Hawaiʻi's Deaf community shows this almost diagrammatically.

Click the three bars in the panel.

The middle layer has a name: CHSL, Creole Hawaiʻi Sign Language — a blend used by roughly forty people who grew up between the HSL world and the ASL world. It is not broken HSL and it is not accented ASL. It is what happens when a community builds a bridge out of two languages.

Linguists used to treat mixtures like this as decay. Modern work treats them as creations in their own right — and CHSL will probably outlast the language it came from.

A language with no sound

…and no less of a language for it. Thank you! Questions?

Hawai'i Sign Language was invisible for a century, not because it was hidden, but because nobody looked. It took a Deaf woman insisting, for years, that what her elders were doing with their hands was not a worse version of somebody else's language.

If a language can hide in plain sight in Honolulu, ask yourself what else is out there — and who is not being believed about it.
