Burushaski

بُرُݸشَسْکِݵ

A mountain language with no proven relatives anywhere on Earth

Who and where

"Our language"

  • Speakers, the Burusho, also call it miśáaski, "our language."
  • It is spoken in the Hunza, Nagar and Yasin valleys of Gilgit-Baltistan, in Pakistan-administered northern Kashmir.
  • A small community also lives in Srinagar, in Indian-administered Kashmir.
  • Estimates reach well over 100,000 speakers, which is many for this series. So why is it in danger? Keep watching.

A juicy clue: Modern Burushaski says báalt for "apple." Linguist Hermann Berger suggested that the ancient word behind English "apple" may have been borrowed from an ancestor of Burushaski. It's an intriguing theory, not settled fact.

You can't just say "hand"

Body parts and family words must show whose they are. In Burushaski, there's no ordinary everyday word for "a hand" or "a mother" alone.

Count in twenties

English counts mainly in tens. Burushaski counts in twenties: 60 is iskí-áltar, "three twenties." French does something similar with 80, quatre-vingts, "four twenties."

Today

About 300 to 350 speakers keep a distinct variety alive, shaped by Kashmiri, Urdu and Hindi.

Community elder Raja Jehangir Ali said that even when only two Burusho people are in a gathering, they prefer to speak their own language.

Why it matters now

Thousands of speakers can still lose a language

  • The risk: speakers often use several languages and increasingly shift to Urdu. Few people read and write their first language.
  • Writing: In the 1940s, poet Nasiruddin Hunzai adapted Urdu script and designed new letters for Burushaski sounds.
  • Books: A three-volume Burushaski-Urdu dictionary appeared between 2006 and 2013.
  • 2023: The group Burusho Maraka launched a primer for reading and writing, and asked for local-language schooling.
  • Online: Researchers share recordings, and speakers can contribute to the Lost Burushaski dictionary.
