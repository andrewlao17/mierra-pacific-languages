Manchu

ᠮᠠᠨᠵᡠ ᡤᡳᠰᡠᠨ

The language of China's last empire

In Manchu it is called manju gisun, "the Manchu language." Its script runs top to bottom.

ᠮᠠᠨᠵᡠ

Who and where

A northern language

  • A Tungusic language from Manchuria, in northeast China. It grew out of Jurchen, the language of an earlier northern people.
  • It was the national language of the Qing dynasty (1644–1912), China's last imperial dynasty.
  • About 10 million people are Manchu today, but almost all speak Mandarin Chinese.
  • UNESCO lists Manchu as critically endangered: only a handful of elderly native speakers remain, though thousands now study it.
Heilongjiang, northeast China

Language of power

An empire on paper

  • Early Qing rulers spoke mostly Manchu, and it became the language of the court.
  • Officials wrote millions of documents in Manchu. Historians still use them, because some contain information found nowhere in Chinese.
  • Palace signs in the Forbidden City are bilingual: Chinese and Manchu side by side.
  • In the Manchu version of a 1689 treaty with Russia, China is called Dulimbai gurun, "the central country."

The script

Top to bottom, left to right

  • In 1599, the Jurchen leader Nurhaci ordered a script made for his people's language, adapted from the Mongolian alphabet.
  • Manchu is written in vertical columns, read top to bottom, with columns going left to right.
  • It's an alphabet: letters join together and change shape depending on where they fall in a word.

Dots and circles

The first Manchu letters were borrowed from Mongolian, where some different sounds looked identical. In 1632, the scholar Dahai is credited with adding dots and circles to tell them apart.
