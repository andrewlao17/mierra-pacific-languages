Jarawa

the Ang · Andaman Islands, India

A few hundred people who kept the outside world at a distance until 1998 — and a road, built illegally through their forest, that a court ordered closed in 2002 and that is still carrying tourists today

Watch the road. The headlights are starting.

A note on the pictures in this module. There are none of Jarawa people. Photographs of them circulate widely, most taken without consent and many taken from vehicles on the road you can see behind these slides. Looking at Jarawa people is the harm this module is about, so we are not going to do it.

Where and who

The people who call themselves "us"

  • They live on the western side of South and Middle Andaman Island, in a reserve of around 1,028 square kilometres of dense evergreen forest, as hunters, gatherers and fishers.
  • Population estimates run from about 270 to 500. A survey in 2001–02 recorded 266 speakers — around 70% of the ethnic population at the time.
  • They call themselves Ang, meaning roughly "we people" or "human being." "Jarawa" is not their word. It comes from Aka-Bea, the language of a neighbouring people who were traditionally their enemies, and means something like "people to be afraid of."
  • The Andamanese peoples are among the oldest continuously settled populations outside Africa. Precise dates are genuinely disputed — you will see everything from 26,000 to 55,000 years — but the depth is not.

This module uses "Jarawa" because that is the name in every source, law and news report. Keep in mind whose word it is.

Two words you already know

The Jarawa bow is aao; the iron-tipped arrow is aetaho. These are among the handful of Jarawa words that appear in general reference sources.

That is the state of public knowledge of this language: a few nouns for objects outsiders found interesting. There is no published dictionary a student can open.

The family tree

Two unrelated families, one small archipelago

The Andaman Islands are about 350 km long. They contain — or contained — two entirely unrelated language families, as different from each other as English is from Chinese.

  • Great Andamanese: once around ten languages across the northern and central islands.
  • Ongan: Jarawa and Önge, and presumably also the unknown language of North Sentinel Island, whose people have refused all contact.
  • Neither family has any demonstrated relatives anywhere on earth. Click the tree.

One linguist, Juliette Blevins, has argued that Ongan is a long-lost sister of Proto-Austronesian — the family you met in the Yami, Kavalan and Enggano modules. It is a minority view and most specialists are unconvinced, but it shows what is at stake: these languages may hold information about very deep human history.

Language lab

How do you prove two languages are cousins?

You cannot do it by asking the speakers, and you cannot do it from a map. You do it the way the Pazeh, Enggano and Koro modules did it: by lining up basic vocabulary and looking for regular correspondences.

Jarawa and Önge are spoken on different islands by peoples with very little contact — and the words line up. Click the rows.

  • Notice the pattern: Önge tends to carry a final vowel where Jarawa has dropped it. One rule, applied consistently.
  • That regularity is what separates real relatedness from coincidence. Any two languages share a few lookalikes; only related ones share a system.

Look at the third row especially. Both languages have a shared inherited word meaning "foreigner" — which tells you that the distinction between us and outsiders is older than either language.

The warning next door

What happened to the Great Andamanese

Everything the Jarawa face has already happened, once, on the same islands, within recorded history.

  • In 1858 the British established a penal colony at Port Blair. The Great Andamanese — perhaps 5,000 people speaking around ten related languages — made contact.
  • Within a few decades, introduced disease had destroyed them. Measles, influenza, syphilis and pneumonia swept through a population with no acquired immunity. By the mid-20th century a few dozen remained.
  • The languages went with the people. In 2010, Boa Sr, the last speaker of Bo, died. Present-day Great Andamanese is a mixture of what survived, spoken by a tiny handful of people.

The Jarawa kept outsiders out — sometimes violently — for the whole of that period. That is the single reason they are still here.

The turning point

1998: they came out

  • For most of the 19th and 20th centuries the Jarawa met intruders — loggers, poachers, settlers, officials — with arrows. That reputation protected their forest better than any law did.
  • From the mid-1990s this changed. Jarawa individuals began visiting settlements on the edge of the reserve, at first in small numbers, and from 1998 contact became regular and friendly.
  • Why is genuinely uncertain. The road, the pressure on their forest and its honey, government contact parties handing out bananas and coconuts, and curiosity are all part of the picture.
  • The consequences arrived fast. Measles outbreaks followed within a year or two. Today some Jarawa use jetties, markets and hospitals near the road, and some children have turned up at settler schools asking to be taught.

Note what this is not. It is not a people being dragged out of the forest. It is a people making choices, under pressure, with very little information about what the choices cost.

Why does disease hit so hard?
Resistance to common infections is built up across generations of exposure. A population that has been separate for thousands of years has none, so an illness that a settler shrugs off can kill. This is not a theory: it is the documented history of the Great Andamanese, the Amazon and the Americas.

What about the honey?
Jarawa honey-collecting is renowned, and honey is a major part of both diet and exchange. Outsiders poaching in the reserve take honey, game and fish. A hunter-gatherer economy that is being quietly emptied gives people a very practical reason to come to the road.

Are they "uncontacted"?
Not any more, and the word was always wrong. The Jarawa knew exactly who was outside their forest; they chose the terms of engagement. Their neighbours on North Sentinel Island still do. "Uncontacted" means "has decided against it."

The road

A highway through a reserve

The Andaman Trunk Road was built in the 1970s, straight through a reserve that had been created to protect the Jarawa. It pushed the Middle Andaman Jarawa westward and cut their forest in two.

It has been the subject of litigation for over twenty years. Step along it in the panel.

An honest note on the other side: the road is the main land link for settler communities in the islands, who use it for hospitals, schools and supplies. The alternative is a sea route. This is a real conflict of interests, not a cartoon — which is why it has taken decades and is still unresolved.

What the road carries

"Human safaris"

  • Convoys of vehicles travel the road through the reserve, carrying tourists who hope to see Jarawa people. The practice has been widely documented and is known, in the campaign against it, as the human safari.
  • In 2012, a video published by The Observer appeared to show Jarawa women being instructed to dance for tourists. It caused an international outcry; the Indian government ordered a crackdown; the matter was raised in the British Parliament; a police officer was arrested.
  • The islands receive on the order of 200,000 tourists a year. Rules on photographing or interacting with Jarawa people exist and are repeatedly reported as being broken.
  • The harms are not only about dignity. Every vehicle is a disease vector, every handout changes an economy, and documented cases include sexual exploitation of Jarawa women.

The reason this module shows you no photographs should now be clear. A picture of a Jarawa person taken from a moving vehicle is not a document. It is the thing itself.

Why does it keep happening?

Because there is money in it, the road is the practical route north, enforcement is thin, and the demand does not go away. Tour operators do not advertise "human safari"; they advertise a drive, and what happens on it is left to chance and to drivers.

Discuss: who is responsible here — the tourist in the vehicle, the operator, the local administration, the Supreme Court, or the demand itself? Rank them, and defend your order.

The hardest question in this course

Isolation, integration, or self-determination?

In the course of the litigation, India's Supreme Court reportedly asked the island authorities a blunt question: do you intend to keep the Jarawa isolated, or to attempt to integrate them into mainstream society?

That is a real policy fork, and every option carries a cost. Work through the three in the panel before deciding what you think.

India's official Jarawa policy favours allowing the Jarawa to decide their own pace of engagement, rather than assimilation. The gap between a written policy and a road carrying tourists through a reserve is the subject of this entire module.
