Rare Languages of the Asia-Pacific · Module 29

Rapa Nui

Easter Island · Isla de Pascua · Chile

The most isolated inhabited island on earth, a Polynesian language under pressure from Spanish — and a script that nobody alive can read

Watch the horizon: the sun is rising behind the moai, and a line of rongorongo is scrolling past your feet.

Where and who

The last place anyone reached

  • Rapa Nui sits alone in the southeastern Pacific. Polynesian voyagers settled it between about 1150 and 1280 CE — one of the last habitable places on earth that humans found.
  • The language is Eastern Polynesian, a cousin of Māori, Hawaiian, Tahitian and Marquesan. Those cousins are thousands of kilometres away, and none of them is mutually intelligible with Rapa Nui.
  • Around 6,600 people identify as Rapa Nui; the island's population is over 7,700. Estimates of speakers run from about 1,000 to 2,500, depending on who counts and how.
  • UNESCO lists Rapa Nui as severely endangered. The competing language is not English — it is Spanish, the official language of Chile since the island's annexation in 1888.

Greeting on the island: ʻIorana. You will hear it constantly — one Rapa Nui word that everybody, including visitors, still uses.

Rapa Nui, Valparaíso Region, Chile
Why isolation matters twice

Isolation let Rapa Nui develop its own character over centuries — and, almost certainly, its own writing system, which no other Polynesian society produced.

The same isolation made the island catastrophically vulnerable the moment outsiders arrived with ships, diseases and shackles. Both halves of this module come from the same geography.

The family

Cousins across an ocean

Polynesian languages are unusually close relatives — the whole family spread across the Pacific in a few thousand years, so the resemblances are still obvious even between islands that are nine thousand kilometres apart.

The script

Rongorongo

  • Outsiders first noticed it in 1864: rows of tiny carved figures — birds, fish, people, plants — incised into wooden tablets and objects.
  • Exactly 27 objects survive. They carry roughly 15,000 characters made from over 400 distinct glyphs. None of them is on Rapa Nui. They are in museums in Europe, the Americas and elsewhere.
  • Rongorongo had a place in island life: there were tuhunga tā, specialists who carved and recited, and a chief named Ngaʻara — the last great master of the script, who died around 1859 — ran a rongorongo school and held an annual festival at Anakena where tablets were recited from dawn to dusk.
  • If rongorongo was invented on the island without outside influence, it is one of a tiny handful of times in human history that writing was invented from scratch — and the most recent.

A 2024 radiocarbon study dated four tablets. Three gave 19th-century ranges; one came from wood felled between 1493 and 1509, long before any European arrived. Important caveat: that dates the wood, not the carving.

A row of rongorongo

Stylised shapes, drawn for this module — the real glyphs are far more intricate, and each one is a distinct, repeatable sign.

Think about what 400 distinct signs implies. Alphabets have 20–40. Syllable systems have 50–100. Four hundred suggests something that mixes sounds and whole words — which is also what makes it hard to crack.

Try it

You have to turn the tablet over

Rongorongo is written in reverse boustrophedon. You start at the bottom left and read along the line. When you reach the end, the next line is upside down — so you physically rotate the whole tablet 180 degrees and keep going.

The word boustrophedon means "as the ox turns" — the way a plough goes up one furrow and back down the next. Several ancient writing systems did something like this. Rongorongo is the version that makes you turn the page in your hands.

Use the panel. Read a line, rotate, read the next.

This is one of the few things about rongorongo that is firmly established. The direction of reading is known. What the signs mean is not.

Think like a decipherer

What it takes to crack a script

Egyptian hieroglyphs, Linear B and Maya glyphs were all deciphered within the last two centuries. Rongorongo has defeated everyone. That is not because it is cleverer — it is because the usual tools are missing.

The year everything broke

1862

  • Beginning in 1862, Peruvian slave raiders abducted around 1,500 people from Rapa Nui — a large share of the island's population — to work in Peru. Most died there.
  • International pressure forced a repatriation. Only a handful survived the return journey, and they brought smallpox and tuberculosis back with them.
  • The people taken were not a random sample. Raiders took the able-bodied, and that included chiefs, priests and the literate specialists who carved and recited rongorongo. The script stopped being produced in the late 1860s.
  • By the 1870s the island's population had fallen to roughly a hundred people. Everything that survives of pre-contact Rapa Nui knowledge passed through that bottleneck.

This is the real answer to "why can't we read rongorongo?" Not that it is uncrackable. That the people who could read it were taken away and died, within about a decade of outsiders noticing the script existed.

The numbers

A language sorted by age

In 2016 Chile's education ministry and UNESCO ran a sociolinguistic survey on the island. The results are the clearest picture of language shift in this entire course, because they break it down by age.

  • About 70% of elders speak Rapa Nui fluently. About 35% of people aged 20–39. About 6.7% of children under 12.
  • Half of all speakers are over 40. When the 20–39 group become parents, most do not pass the language on — and in families where one parent is not Rapa Nui, the household language is almost always Spanish.
  • Forty years ago, about 76% of schoolchildren spoke Rapa Nui. Today roughly 10% of young people have real command of it.

Notice that this collapse happened after the language was introduced as a school subject in 1976. Teaching a language for a few hours a week does not, on its own, stop a shift.

Why it matters now

Back from the brink, maybe

  • One school on the island, Lorenzo Baeza Vega, runs a Rapa Nui immersion programme from pre-primary to fourth grade. It has existed for roughly two decades, and the hours allotted to it rise and fall — partly because the school is under pressure to score well on Chile's national standardised tests, which are in Spanish.
  • Preschool immersion has expanded through the Honga'a Reo programme, modelled on the Māori kōhanga reo "language nests" — the approach that pulled Māori back from a similar position.
  • The island has its own language academy, ʻŪmaŋa Hatu Reʻo, and the language is loudly, publicly used at Tapati Rapa Nui, the annual February festival of song, dance, sport and storytelling.
  • Attitudes have changed completely. Forty years ago Rapa Nui was something to leave behind; today it is a point of pride, and that shift is the precondition for everything else.

The remaining obstacle is arithmetic, not attitude: a tourism economy that runs in Spanish and English, a school system measured in Spanish, and a generation of parents who were not given the language themselves.

Does immersion work?
It is the only approach with a track record. Māori and Hawaiian were both in Rapa Nui's position and both recovered child speakers through language nests and immersion schooling. Neither is "saved" — but both have thousands of young speakers who would not otherwise exist.

What does tourism do?
Both things at once. It funds the island and gives Rapa Nui culture an audience and a value — and it fills the island with people who speak Spanish and English, making those the languages of work. Several modules in this course show the same double edge.

And rongorongo?
Still undeciphered, and all 27 objects are still off the island. Researchers continue to analyse the glyph sequences statistically, and the 2024 dating strengthened the case for independent invention. Some Rapa Nui argue the tablets should come home — a question about ownership as much as about linguistics.

ʻIorana

Hello, and goodbye. Thank you! Questions?

A people on the loneliest island on earth invented writing, and then had the readers taken from them by ship. We have the tablets. We do not have the voices that could have read them aloud.

Which is exactly the argument for every other module in this course: record the speakers while the speakers are here.
