Rare Languages of the Asia-Pacific · Module 21

Yami / Tao

ciriciring no Tao · 達悟語 · Pongso no Tao

Taiwan's only indigenous language that doesn't belong to Taiwan's language family — spoken on a volcanic island whose calendar is set by a fish

Watch the sea: the flying fish are arriving, and the night is turning to dawn.

Where and who

An island that faces south

  • Tao means "person." The island is Pongso no Tao, the island of people, and the language is ciriciring no Tao, human speech. Everything is named from the inside out.
  • About 4,700 Tao people, roughly 4,000 speakers, on a volcanic island of 46 square kilometres. UNESCO calls the language definitely endangered.
  • Every other indigenous language of Taiwan is Formosan — a deep, ancient branch of Austronesian. Yami is not. It is Malayo-Polynesian, the branch that left Taiwan thousands of years ago and spread across half the world.
  • Its closest relatives are not on Taiwan at all. They are 100 km south, in the Batanes Islands of the Philippines.

"Yami" was the name given by Japanese ethnologists in the early 1900s and is still the official term; many islanders prefer Tao. Both are used — including by Tao people themselves.

Lanyu Township, Taitung County, Taiwan
The family that came back

Austronesian languages began in Taiwan and spread out by sea — to the Philippines, Indonesia, Madagascar, Hawaiʻi, New Zealand. Roughly 800 years ago, one group sailed back north from the Batanes and settled Orchid Island. Oral history, language and genetics all tell the same story.

So Yami is the only language in Taiwan that is a returning cousin rather than a stay-at-home one.

Speak along

Words that cross the strait

If you speak Tagalog or Ilokano, a surprising amount of Yami will look familiar — and Yami speakers and Ivatan speakers from Batanes have historically been able to understand one another.

For centuries the two peoples traded by boat, exchanging pigs, goats and millet for gold, beads and iron, until the voyages stopped about three hundred years ago after a disastrous fight.

Cultural significance

A year measured in fish

  • Every spring the Kuroshio Current brings schools of alibangbang, flying fish, past the island. The Tao year is built around their arrival — not around a date on a paper calendar.
  • The traditional calendar, Ahehep no Tao, splits the year into three seasons defined entirely by what the fish are doing. Click the wheel to visit each.
  • The "Flying Fish Festival" isn't one day. It is a whole season containing roughly eleven separate rituals and three sets of taboos: when to catch, who may eat, how to cook, and when to stop.
  • The origin story is unusual. In most myths a god makes people; in this one a black-winged flying fish appears and gives the Tao a set of rules — and the people who follow them stay well.

Think like a scientist

Good fish, bad fish

The Tao sort every fish in the sea into categories — and the categories decide who in the family eats it. Broadly: oyod, "good fish," go first to women and children; rahet, "bad fish," are eaten by men; and some fish are reserved for the oldest people in the village.

It sounds arbitrary. Look closer. The "good" fish are the shy, delicate reef fish that are hard to catch. The "bad" fish are the slow ones that grab any bait. Researchers who have studied the system argue it works as a fishing quota in disguise: the species that would collapse first under heavy fishing are precisely the ones that only part of the community may eat.

Nobody wrote it down as a conservation policy. It arrived as a set of rules from a fish, and it has run for centuries.

Built for this island

No nails, no screws, no glue

  • A Tao plank boat is assembled from 21 to 27 separate pieces of wood, pegged and lashed together without a single nail. A small tatala carries one to three people; a large cinedkeran carries six to ten.
  • The wood must come from living trees — using dead wood on a boat is taboo — and the trees are often ones the builder's family planted generations earlier. A boat can take years to finish.
  • The painted patterns are not decoration. They say which village and which family the boat belongs to, and the eye on the prow, mata no tatala, watches for fish and keeps bad spirits off. Launching one is one of the great honours of a family's life.
  • Houses are engineered too. Orchid Island sits in the typhoon lane, so the traditional vahay is built partly underground, stepped into the slope, where the strongest wind passes over the roof.

Boats are men's work, and the taboo against women touching them applies to visitors too — something to know before you photograph one.

Grammar of a family

Your name changes when your child is born

In most of the world, a name is fixed at birth and follows you to the grave. On Orchid Island it moves. When a couple's first child is born and named, the parents are renamed after the baby — and when the first grandchild arrives, the grandparents are renamed too.

  • Si- marks a person's own name · Syaman- "father of" · Sinan- "mother of" · Syapen- "grandparent of"
  • Anthropologists call this teknonymy. It exists elsewhere — in Bali, among the Penan of Borneo, in Arabic Abu and Umm names — but few societies carry it as far as the Tao do.
  • Think about what it means: your place in the community is stated, out loud, every time somebody says your name. You are whose parent you are.

Taiwan's best-known Tao writer publishes as Syaman Rapongan — "father of Rapongan." His novels and essays about the sea and about growing up between two worlds are read far beyond the island.

Why it matters now

Nearly 100,000 barrels

  • In 1982, Taiwan's state power company began shipping low-level radioactive waste to Orchid Island. Residents were not told what it was. The site stopped taking new waste in 1996, with close to 100,000 barrels stored on an island of around 5,000 people.
  • The Tao have protested since 1988. Elders describe the barrels in their own terms: as anito, evil spirits, dumped on the island of people.
  • In 2016 the president apologised to Taiwan's indigenous peoples, and a government report later confirmed that the Tao had never consented. In 2019 compensation of around NT$2.55 billion was offered — and refused. Spend it on removal instead, the community said.
  • The waste is still there. No final disposal site has been agreed anywhere in Taiwan.

This is a language module, and this belongs in it. A community fighting for its island's survival is a community with less time for language classes — and a reason to insist on being heard in its own words.

The language today

Layers you can hear

  • Yami carries its history in its vocabulary. Fifty years of Japanese rule left words like gako (school) and sinsi (teacher). Decades of Mandarin schooling left a few more. Play the game and listen for the seams.
  • Two things have helped the language hold on: isolation — it is a two-hour boat ride from anywhere — and the church, which put Yami into writing and uses it in services.
  • There is now a full reference grammar and dictionary, an online dictionary, government teaching materials, and a documentation project with recordings and lessons on the web.
  • And there is literature. Syaman Rapongan writes books in which the sea, not the mainland, is the centre of the world — putting Orchid Island at the middle of the map instead of the edge.
