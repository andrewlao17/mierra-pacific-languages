Kaurna

Kaurna warra · the Adelaide Plains, South Australia

Every module in this course has ended with the same worry: documentation preserves, but only speakers continue. Here is the language that tested whether a community can put that in reverse

Where and who

A city built on top of it

  • Kaurna warra is the language of the Adelaide Plains — the flat, fertile country between the Mount Lofty Ranges and the sea in South Australia. Kaurna meyunna means "Kaurna people."
  • In 1836 the British founded the city of Adelaide directly on that country. Within a human lifetime the language had stopped being spoken as an everyday tongue.
  • The person usually named as the last first-language speaker, Ivaritji, died in 1929. For roughly sixty years after that, nobody spoke Kaurna.
  • Today you will hear it at the opening of events across Adelaide, in schools, in the names of the Park Lands and on university courses. That is not a survival story. It is a reawakening story, and it is different.

Australia had hundreds of languages before 1788. Roughly a dozen are still being learned by children. Dozens more are in the position Kaurna was in — recorded on paper, waiting.

Why this module is last of its kind

Yonaguni, Nivkh, Ket, Rapa Nui — each one still has speakers, and each module ended with the same unanswered question: if the last speaker goes, is that it?

Kaurna went past that point. It has been there and come back. Whatever you think about language revival, this is the case to argue from.

Words matter

"Sleeping," not "extinct"

Linguists used to write that Kaurna was extinct. Many Aboriginal communities reject that word, and the terminology you will see in Australia now is sleeping, dormant or awakening.

This is not politeness. It is a claim about what a language is — and it has practical consequences. Work through the panel.

Notice how the terminology changes what you expect to be possible. If a language is extinct, the only sensible response is to study it. If it is sleeping, the sensible response is to wake it up.

The accidental archive

Two German missionaries, 1838

  • In October 1838, two years after the colony was founded, Christian Teichelmann and Clamor Schürmann arrived in Adelaide — Lutheran missionaries trained in Germany. They set about learning Kaurna properly, working with Kaurna men including Mullawirraburka, Kadlitpinna and Ityamaiitpinna.
  • In December 1839 they opened a school at Piltawodli, on the north bank of the River Torrens, where Kaurna children were taught to read and write in their own language.
  • In 1840 they published a grammar sketch and vocabulary. Across all the historical sources, somewhere between 3,000 and 3,500 Kaurna words were written down — at the time the largest Aboriginal wordlist recorded anywhere in Australia.
  • Their motive was conversion. They translated the Ten Commandments and German hymns, and never finished the Bible. Their legacy turned out to be something else entirely.

Both men also believed colonisation was a menace to the people they lived among, and said so. Within a few years the authorities closed the Kaurna-language school and moved the children into English-only education.

Be the reviver

Is it pudni or budni?

Here is the first problem anyone reviving Kaurna hits. The missionaries wrote the language down using German spelling conventions, by ear, with no recording equipment and no training in phonetics as we know it.

So when their notebooks give two spellings for the same word, you have to work out whether that is two different words, a mistake — or something about Kaurna itself that German ears could not hear.

Speak along

Have a conversation in Kaurna

This is the part that makes Kaurna different from every other module in this course. You are not learning a few words of something dying. You are learning the same greetings that get used in Adelaide schools and at public events every week.

are you good? — hello, to one person

I'm good

The greeting song Niina Marni was composed by Aunty Cherie Warrarra Watkins at Kaurna Plains School, and children across South Australia now learn it.

Kaurna marks how many people you are talking to: niina for one, niwa for two, naa for a group. English lost that distinction centuries ago.

How it was done

It started with a songbook

  • In 1989, Kaurna people and the linguist Rob Amery began working together — not on a dictionary, but on songs. Children at Kaurna Plains School needed something to sing.
  • That is the right order, and it is not obvious. Songs are short, memorable, performable and public. They give people something to do with a language before anyone can hold a conversation in it.
  • From there: school programmes, a university course from 1997, Amery's doctoral thesis on reclaiming Kaurna from written sources, and in 2002 the founding of Kaurna Warra Pintyanthi — "creating Kaurna language" — by elders Alitya Rigney and Lewis Yerloburka O'Brien with Amery.
  • KWP does the slow work: answering requests for names and translations, building teaching materials, and deciding on new words.

Note who leads. The linguist is a collaborator; the authority over the language sits with Kaurna elders and the community body. Revivals that get this backwards tend not to last.

Why songs first?
Because a song can be sung correctly by someone who does not yet speak the language — and singing it in public, to an audience, makes the language real in a way that a wordlist never does. Several Australian revivals have started the same way.

Who owns the language?
The Kaurna community. KWP exists partly so that requests — for a name, a translation, a Welcome to Country — go to a Kaurna-led body rather than to whoever happens to answer the phone. Control over how the language is used is part of the revival, not a side issue.
click to reveal
Is it the same language?
Honestly: it is a reconstruction. Modern Kaurna is based on 19th-century records, filled out with new words, and spoken by people whose first language is English. Linguists distinguish the historical sources from the emerging modern language. Both are Kaurna. Neither is a fake.

Be the committee

The 1840 dictionary has no word for "email"

A vocabulary recorded in 1840 describes the world of 1840. Reviving a language means deciding, deliberately and in public, what to call a phone, a hospital, a bus, a computer and a football club.

Every living language does this constantly — English did it with computer, download and podcast. The difference is that in a revival the decisions are made by a named committee, on the record, instead of by millions of people without noticing.

By the early 1990s more than a hundred new Kaurna terms had already been developed. Click the strategies in the panel, then try the challenge.

There is no "correct" answer at the bottom of the panel — because in real life there isn't one either. The committee argues, decides, and the community either uses the word or quietly doesn't.

The map changes

Putting the names back

The most visible part of the Kaurna revival is not in classrooms. It is on signs. Adelaide City Council and the South Australian government have restored Kaurna names across the city, in most cases as dual names alongside the English ones.

Every one of the Adelaide Park Lands — the ring of green that surrounds the city centre — now carries a Kaurna name. Click the map.

In 2018 the Federal Court recognised Kaurna native title over the Adelaide Plains, reported at the time as the first such determination covering an Australian capital city's metropolitan area.

A name on a sign is a small thing and a large thing at once. It costs a council almost nothing. It also means a child riding past reads a Kaurna word every day without being taught it.

The honest assessment

So — did it work?

It depends entirely on what you were asking for.

  • If you wanted a community of fluent first-language speakers: not yet, and possibly not ever. There is no neighbourhood in Adelaide where Kaurna is the household language, and the modern language is spoken by people who think in English.
  • If you wanted the language used in public life: yes, comprehensively. Welcome to Country, school songs, place names, university courses, a language academy, new words made every year by a Kaurna-led body.
  • If you wanted a model others can follow: absolutely. Kaurna is now one of the most-cited reclamation projects in the world, and dozens of Australian communities have used its methods on their own sources.

Hebrew is the usual comparison: revived from written sources into a national language with millions of speakers. It took a state, an education system, mass migration and a hundred years. Kaurna has a city council, a university department, some schools and about thirty-five years.

The course's question, answered

Every module so far has ended with the same line: documentation preserves, only speakers continue. Kaurna shows that line is not quite right.

Documentation is not a substitute for speakers. But if it is good enough, and the community wants it badly enough, it is a seed. Teichelmann and Schürmann were not trying to save a language for the 21st century. They did it anyway, by writing carefully.
