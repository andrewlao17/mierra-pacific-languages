Rare Languages of the Asia-Pacific · Module 19

Pazeh

Pazih · 巴宰語 · central Taiwan

The only language in Taiwan that counted six as "five-and-one" — and the one whose last native speaker we can still watch on video

Watch the mountains: the mist over the Puli basin lifts a little with every slide.

Where and who

A people the maps forgot

  • The Pazeh lived on the fertile plains of what is now Taichung and Miaoli, in western Taiwan — good farmland, which is exactly why they lost it.
  • Around 1823 the Pazeh and several neighbouring peoples left those plains and resettled in the Puli basin, ringed by mountains in the centre of the island. Most Pazeh today live in the Ailan district of Puli.
  • They are a Pingpu or "plains" people. Taiwan officially recognises 16 indigenous peoples — and the Pazeh are not one of them. They have been applying since 2024.
  • The last native speaker of Pazeh died in 2010. About 12 speakers of the closely related Kaxabu variety were counted in 2013.

Being unrecognised is not a detail: it decides who gets language funding, school programmes and a seat at the table.

Puli, Nantou County, Taiwan
Why did everyone end up in Puli?

In the 1800s, settlers from China took over the western plains through debt, lease and force. In 1823 around a dozen plains peoples — Pazeh, Kaxabu, Taokas, Papora, Babuza and others — made a mass migration into the Puli basin. In December 2023 their descendants gathered in Puli to mark 200 years since that move.

Puli is now a patchwork: several different indigenous peoples, several different languages, several different New Years, all inside one small valley.

The big picture

Taiwan is where it all started

Austronesian is one of the largest language families on earth — from Madagascar off Africa to Rapa Nui in the eastern Pacific, taking in the Philippines, Indonesia, Hawaiʻi and New Zealand. Most linguists trace the whole family back to Taiwan, and the deepest branches are still spoken there. Pazeh was one of them. Click a language to compare words.

The x is a scrapey sound made at the back of the mouth, a bit like the ch in Scottish loch.

Pazeh is the only Formosan language that builds every number from six to nine by adding. Its neighbours mostly multiply (8 as 2 × 4) or subtract (9 as 10 − 1).

One more oddity: xasep "five" doesn't look like the Austronesian word for five, lima, at all. Linguists are still arguing about where it came from.

Grammar you can feel

Six different ways to say "no"

English pushes nearly all its negatives through one word: not. "I'm not going," "that's not a dog," "there's no water," "don't touch." Pazeh splits that job between six separate words, and picking the wrong one is like using the wrong tool.

  • ini plain "not" · uzay "it isn't that one" · kuang "there is none"
  • mayaw "not yet" · nah "don't want to" · ana "don't!"

Negator list from Paul Jen-kuei Li's Pazih dictionary. The English equivalents are rough: no two languages cut up meaning the same way.

Grammar you can see

Three words for "he" and "she"

English has one word for a person you're talking about: she. It doesn't matter whether she's standing next to you or living in another country. Pazeh cares, and has three words — the choice depends on where the person is and whether you can see them.

  • imini — this one, right here
  • imisiw — that one over there, in sight
  • isia — that one, not in sight

Pazeh also splits "we" in two: ita means "we, including you," and yami means "we, not including you." Imagine the arguments English could avoid.

Cultural significance

Ayan: songs that call the ancestors

  • Pazeh and Kaxabu ancestral songs are called ayan, after the word they begin by calling out. Each verse addresses the ancestors directly, then carries a piece of history: where the people came from, who led them, what was lost.
  • That matters enormously for a people with almost no written records. For generations, the song was the archive — the land claim, the family tree and the history book all at once.
  • The Pazeh New Year falls on the 15th day of the 11th lunar month, when families gather and serve umu, a pounded rice dish made for the occasion.
  • Christian missionaries reached the area in 1865 and most Pazeh had converted by 1871. Many old rituals faded — but the missionaries' romanised alphabet became the way Pazeh got written down, and the church is now the centre of the language revival.
Why sing it?
An ayan verse is easy to carry and hard to confiscate. Songs survived two colonial governments, a mass migration and a century of official erasure.

One person

Pan Jin-yu, 1914–2010

  • She was born in Puli in 1914 to Kaxabu parents and raised by Pazeh-speaking adoptive parents in Ailan. She trained as a nurse. For most of her life the language she actually used day to day was Taiwanese Hokkien — Pazeh had no one left to speak it with.
  • In her eighties and nineties she became her people's teacher, running Pazeh classes for about 200 regular students in Puli, plus smaller groups in Miaoli and Taichung.
  • Two of her students, both in their sixties and seventies, won national awards for poetry written in Pazeh — five years after their first lesson.
  • She died on 24 October 2010, aged 96. The last conversation she recorded with a linguist was published as a paper titled "The Last Text of the Last Pazih Speaker."
What one person left behind: click an item.

A language doesn't vanish all at once. What survives depends entirely on what got written, recorded and taught while someone still knew it.

Pazeh is usually listed as extinct from 2010. Some community members say a few fluent speakers of the related Kaxabu variety remain.

Why it matters now

Can a language come back?

  • This is the hard version of the question. With Yonaguni or Yaeyama there are still elders to learn from. With Pazeh, the teachers are a dictionary, a song collection and a set of recordings.
  • It isn't hopeless. Hebrew, Cornish and Wampanoag were all revived from written records by people determined enough — but all of them took decades, and they needed the records to exist first.
  • In Puli, the Ailan Presbyterian Church runs weekend classes, a Pazeh cultural association was founded in 2003, and community members keep writing new poems in the language.
