Rare Languages of the Asia-Pacific · Module 26

Koro Aka

East Kameng, Arunachal Pradesh, India

In 2010 the world's language count went up by one. Not because a new language was created — because linguists walked into a village and realised two languages were being spoken there, and nobody had noticed

Watch the hillside: the village lights are coming on. One of them is not like the others.

Where and who

A thousand people in the hills

  • Arunachal Pradesh is India's northeasternmost state: forested Himalayan foothills on the borders of Bhutan, Tibet and Myanmar, with dozens of languages packed into valleys that are hard to reach.
  • In the Kameng districts live the Aka, also called Hruso — four to six thousand people, farmers and hunters in villages of stilt houses.
  • Among them, in the same villages, in the same tribe, live the Koro — somewhere between 800 and 1,500 people, who speak something else entirely.
  • Koro is unwritten, passed on by speaking. Few speakers are under twenty, and many younger Koro now use Hindi as their main language.

Visiting Arunachal Pradesh requires a government permit even for Indian citizens from other states. That restriction is one reason its languages have gone unrecorded for so long.

East Kameng District, Arunachal Pradesh, India
Two languages, one village

This is the unusual part. Koro speakers are not a separate tribe living down the valley. They are members of the Aka tribe — same community, same festivals, same chief, intermarried for generations — who speak a language their neighbours cannot understand.

And until 2010, both communities described the difference as a matter of accent.

Be the linguist

How you find a hidden language

In 2008 a team from the National Geographic Enduring Voices project — Gregory Anderson, David Harrison and Ganesh Murmu — went to Arunachal Pradesh to record two little-documented languages, Aka and Miji.

They worked the way linguists do: house to house, collecting a standard word list. Body parts, numbers, animals, landscape. And partway through, the words stopped matching.

The speakers had told them it was the same language with a slight difference in accent. The notebook said otherwise. Use the panel and see what they saw.

This is why word lists exist. They are boring, repetitive, and they catch things that conversation and good manners hide.

The measurement

Nine words in a hundred

When linguists want to know how close two speech varieties are, one quick test is to compare basic vocabulary and count the matches. It is rough, but it sorts the obvious cases.

  • As a rule of thumb, two dialects of one language share most of their basic words — commonly 80% or more.
  • Koro and Hruso Aka, spoken in the same villages by people of the same tribe, share about 9%.
  • Nine percent is not "a strong accent." Nine percent is roughly what you would expect from two languages that have been apart for thousands of years, or were never together at all.

The surveys and censuses that list India's languages had never recorded Koro separately. In 2010 the published world total went from 6,909 languages to 6,910 — one line added to a list, for a language with a full grammar, a vocabulary and a thousand speakers.

The mystery

So where did Koro come from?

A language does not simply appear inside another community. Either the Koro moved there, or the Aka arrived around them, or something else happened. Three explanations are on the table, and the evidence does not cleanly pick one.

The bigger picture

The least-mapped language region on earth

Koro was not a freak event. Arunachal Pradesh has been called the black hole of linguistics, and it has earned it: few linguists have worked there, a permit is required to enter, and no reliable list of the state's languages has ever been completed.

What is there is extraordinary. Several small language families sit side by side in one state — and researchers increasingly suspect that some of them may not belong to Sino-Tibetan at all, but could be survivors of older families with no surviving relatives.

Click the families in the panel. The question marks are not laziness. They are the honest state of the science.

If several independent families really are hiding in one Indian state, this region matters for the history of all of Asia — and most of those languages have a few thousand speakers each.

Identity

Aka by tribe, Koro by tongue

We tend to assume that a people and a language come as a pair: the French speak French, the Japanese speak Japanese. Koro is a reminder that the world is not organised that neatly.

  • Koro speakers say they are Aka. They share the tribe's festivals, institutions and history, and they intermarry.
  • Linguistically they speak something only distantly related to Aka — perhaps not related at all.
  • Both statements are true at once. The community's own description is not wrong; it is answering a different question than the linguist's.

Linguists also note that this arrangement usually kills the smaller language. A minority tongue inside a shared tribal identity has no separate flag to rally around — which makes Koro's survival this long genuinely surprising.

Two true answers to "who are you?"

The community's answer

We are Aka. We live in Aka villages, we keep Aka custom, our chief is the Aka chief. The way we talk is just a little different.

The linguist's answer

These are two separate languages. They share about nine words in a hundred, and the sound systems are built differently.

Discuss: who gets to decide what counts as a separate language — the people who speak it, or the scientists who measure it? What is at stake in the answer? (Hint: government recognition, school funding and census categories all depend on it.)

Cultural significance

Houses on stilts, silver, and spirit houses

  • The Aka live in villages of houses raised on stilts, farming, hunting and keeping livestock in steep forested country. Villages elect a chief who serves as headman.
  • The big Aka festival is Nechido, held over four days in November under the chief's direction — a community occasion tied to the natural world.
  • Dress matters here. Silver ornaments are an important part of women's traditional costume, and facial tattooing has been practised among some Aka.
  • The Koro keep their own ritual practice within this shared world: documentation teams photographed a Koro priest performing a ritual in front of a sokrou ngin, a spirit house, in Pichong village.

Centuries of contact with Assam and with Buddhist neighbours have layered Hindu and Buddhist influence over older indigenous belief — one more reason the region's history is hard to untangle.

Who did the fieldwork?
Not just the visiting linguists. The published work names Koro and Hruso consultants — Sange Degio, Khandu Degio, Anthony Degio and others — who did the slow work of giving words, checking transcriptions and listening to playback. A language is "discovered" by the people who already knew it.

What does "discovered" even mean?
A thousand people had been speaking Koro their whole lives. Nothing was discovered to them. What happened in 2010 is that the language entered the written record of the outside world — which matters because census categories, school policy and funding all run on that record.

Why does the permit matter?
The Inner Line Permit restricts entry to Arunachal Pradesh. It was designed to protect indigenous communities from outside pressure, and it has done some of that. It has also meant that almost no linguists went in — so the state's languages have gone unrecorded in a way that is now a race against time.

Why it matters now

Endangered the day it was found

The researchers described the discovery as bittersweet, and they were right to. Koro entered the world's language catalogue already in trouble: a few hundred to a thousand speakers, almost none of them children, in a community where the prestige languages are Aka, Hindi and English.

  • A follow-up expedition in 2011 found something more hopeful: speakers aged 16 to 20 who still used the language. Not many, but not zero.
  • Recordings now exist — stories, word lists, audio placed online — where in 2007 there were none at all.
  • And Koro now has a name in print. That sounds trivial; it is not. A language that does not appear in any official list cannot be taught in a school, counted in a census or funded by anyone.

The deeper lesson for a class is this: the count of the world's languages is not a fact we possess. It is an estimate, and it is wrong in both directions — there are languages on the list that no longer exist, and languages like Koro that were never on it.
