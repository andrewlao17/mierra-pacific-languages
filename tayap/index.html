Tayap

Gapun village · East Sepik Province, Papua New Guinea

One village. Fewer than two hundred people. Fewer than fifty speakers. No ban, no war, no school that forbade it — and everyone in the village says they want their children to speak it

Watch the speech bubbles. They are changing colour.

Where and who

A village in a swamp

  • Gapun sits in dense rainforest and mangrove swamp near the mouth of the Sepik River, in East Sepik Province. There is no road. Getting there means a canoe, a long walk through mud, or both.
  • The village has fewer than 200 people. Tayap is spoken nowhere else on earth, and never has been.
  • It is best treated as a language isolate — no demonstrated relatives. Some classifications group it loosely with neighbouring families, but nobody has shown a relationship. For all anyone knows, it is as old as Greek or Chinese.
  • Gapun society is matrilineal, unlike most of its neighbours — one of several ways this small village is its own world.

Everything in this module comes from one extraordinary body of work: the anthropologist Don Kulick first came to Gapun in 1985 and returned for over thirty years, eventually publishing both a grammar and dictionary of Tayap and a book for general readers about its death.

Why study this village?

Kulick went looking for a place where he could watch a language die — not reconstruct it afterwards, but observe the process while it was happening, over decades, in one community small enough to know entirely.

Gapun turned out to be almost ideal for that, which is a bleak thing to be good at. It is also why this module can answer a question none of the others can: what does language death actually look like from the inside?

The context

The most multilingual country on earth

Papua New Guinea has somewhere around 800 to 1,000 languages — not dialects, separate languages — in a country of roughly eight million people, on an area about the size of California.

That is somewhere between a tenth and an eighth of all the languages in the world, in one country with about a thousandth of its population.

And very many of them are like Tayap: spoken by a few hundred people, in one place, with no written tradition and no relatives anyone can identify. Press the button in the panel.

Rugged terrain, dense forest and small, independent communities are the usual explanation: for thousands of years, the next valley was far enough away to be a different world.

The real mechanism

What the two languages came to mean

Kulick's finding, after thirty years, is that nobody in Gapun chose to abandon Tayap. What happened instead is that the two languages picked up meanings — associations nobody states out loud and most people would deny.

The trap

"They pounce"

Here is the detail that should matter most to anyone who cares about saving a language.

The older, fluent speakers of Tayap want young people to speak it. They say so. And when a young person tries — stumbles over a word, mispronounces something — the fluent speakers correct them immediately and publicly.

Which means that trying to speak Tayap, in Gapun, carries a guaranteed cost: embarrassment. Speaking Tok Pisin carries none. Run the conversation in the panel and watch what happens.

Nobody is being cruel. Everybody is doing something reasonable. The outcome is that the young people stop trying — and then the elders say that young people don't care about the language.

Kulick found something else: when he got young people alone in his house and asked them to tell stories, several of them turned out to be surprisingly fluent. They could speak it. They just wouldn't, in front of anyone.

The arithmetic

Ninety to forty-five, in one lifetime

  • When Kulick first arrived in the mid-1980s, about 90 people in Gapun spoke Tayap. By the time he finished, it was about 45 — in a village of fewer than 200.
  • Half the speakers, gone in thirty years. Not through death alone: through children growing up who never acquired it.
  • The youngest speakers are in their early thirties — and they essentially never use the language in daily life.
  • There is no generation below them. Which means that unless something changes, the arithmetic finishes itself.

One consolation Kulick offers: some words will outlive the language. Names for birds and plants that have no equivalent in Tok Pisin will survive inside Tok Pisin sentences, long after nobody can build a Tayap sentence around them.

The other language

What is replacing it is also a survivor

It would be easy to cast Tok Pisin as the villain. It isn't one — and understanding why is the most useful thing in this module.

  • Tok Pisin is itself a creole, forged on colonial plantations where workers speaking dozens of unrelated languages had to communicate. Its vocabulary comes mostly from English; its grammar does not. You met exactly this process in the Palenquero and Kristang modules.
  • It is now one of Papua New Guinea's official languages and the most widely spoken language in the country — the thing that lets a nation with 800 languages hold a conversation with itself.
  • For a young person in Gapun, Tok Pisin is church, radio, school, town, money, the possibility of leaving. Tayap is the village. Nobody has to be persuaded.

So the story is not a big language crushing a small one. It is a language born out of colonial exploitation, which became a tool of national unity, now quietly dissolving the languages it was built to connect. Nothing in that is anyone's plan.

Where did Tok Pisin come from?
From plantations, where labourers recruited from all over the region were deliberately mixed so they could not organise. The pidgin they built to talk to each other became a full language when children grew up speaking it. The same story as Palenquero, three centuries later and an ocean away.

Did it only replace Tayap?
No — and this is often missed. Gapun villagers used to speak several neighbouring languages in order to deal with their neighbours. Tok Pisin replaced all of that multilingualism too. The village did not go from one language to two. It went from many to fewer.
click to reveal
What about English?
English sits above even Tok Pisin in prestige. Kulick found young men in Gapun writing love letters studded with English words they did not understand — because English signalled sophistication. A language can exert pressure on a community that does not speak it.

Cultural significance

A whole world, in one clearing

  • Gapun is matrilineal — descent and belonging run through mothers — which sets it apart from most of its neighbours and is bound up with how the village is organised.
  • In Gapun, nobody dies a natural death. Every death is understood to have a cause, usually sorcery, and working out who was responsible is a serious communal process. That belief system has its own vocabulary, its own oratory and its own rules about who may say what.
  • Christianity arrived and was absorbed on local terms — tangled up with ideas about cargo, literacy and the sources of white people's wealth. Reading and writing were not neutral skills; they were understood as a route to transformation.
  • All of that happens in language. Not the dictionary part — the oratory, the insult, the joke, the correct way to accuse someone. When those genres go, something goes that a wordlist cannot hold.

Kulick's book spends as much time on personalities as on grammar, deliberately. Gapun is not a case study. It is a few dozen people who argue, flirt, hold grudges and make each other laugh.

What a grammar can't record

A dictionary can hold the word for a bird. A grammar can hold how to build a sentence. Neither can hold:

— how to make a public accusation of sorcery without naming anyone
— what makes a joke funny in this village
— the right register for talking to your mother's brother
— the tone that means you are not actually angry

These are the parts of a language that die first and are documented last, if at all.

Thank you! Questions?

The villagers of Gapun say they love Tayap and want their children to speak it. They say it in Tok Pisin. Every person in this story is acting reasonably, and the language is going anyway.

That is the version of language death you should carry out of this course — not a villain, but a slow accumulation of sensible choices. It is also the reason the Kaurna, Kristang and Palenquero modules matter: reversing it takes a decision, because losing it never did.
