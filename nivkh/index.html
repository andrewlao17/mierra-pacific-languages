Nivkh

Нивхгу диф · Nivhgu dif · Amur & Sakhalin

A language with no known relatives on earth — and a word for "house" that changes its first sound depending on who owns it

Watch the river: the ice is breaking up and starting to move.

Where and who

River mouth and island

  • The Nivkh live where the Amur River meets the sea, and on the northern half of Sakhalin Island, in the Russian Far East. Winters are long; the river freezes solid and then breaks up with a roar every spring.
  • They call themselves nivhgu, "people," and their language nivhgu dif. Outsiders long called them Gilyak, a borrowed name.
  • Here is the striking part. The number of Nivkh people has barely changed in 120 years — about 4,500 in 1897 and about 4,650 in 2010. The number of speakers collapsed from essentially everyone to 198.
  • The people are still here. The language is the thing that is disappearing — and those are not the same problem.

Counts vary by what is measured. Some specialists put the number of genuinely fluent speakers today at fewer than 30.

How languages are related

A branch with no tree

Most languages have cousins. English has German and Dutch; Japanese has the Ryukyuan languages; Tagalog has hundreds of Austronesian relatives. Nivkh has none that anyone can prove. Linguists call this a language isolate.

That is not for lack of trying. Nivkh has been linked to Tungusic, Korean, Chukotko-Kamchatkan, Mongolic, Ainu, and even languages of North America. Click each proposal to see how it has fared.

Resemblances alone are not evidence. To prove relatedness you need regular sound correspondences — a pattern where a sound in one language matches a specific sound in the other, again and again, across hundreds of words. Nobody has found that for Nivkh.

Isolates matter enormously. Each one is an independent sample of what a human language can be. There are only a few dozen left.

Language lab

The word that changes shape

The Nivkh word for house is тыф (təf). But you will almost never hear it that way, because the moment you put another word in front of it, its first sound changes — and which change you get depends on how the previous word ends.

Welsh and Irish do something similar; almost nothing else does. Try all five in the panel and watch the т transform.

  • Previous word ends in a vowel → the hard stop softens: т → р
  • Previous word ends in a fricative or trill → it stays hard: т → т
  • Previous word ends in a nasal (sometimes one you can't even hear any more) → it becomes voiced: т → д

This is why the language is hard to learn from a dictionary. A dictionary lists təf — but in real speech the word mostly isn't təf.

Counting

Thirty-three ways to say "two"

Nivkh does not have one set of numbers. It has at least thirty-three. The number word takes a different ending depending on what you are counting — and the categories tell you exactly what mattered in life on the Amur.

English has a faint echo of this: a pair of shoes, a head of cattle, a sheet of paper. Nivkh built it into the numerals themselves, mostly for the numbers one to five.

Classifier counts from Gruzdeva's description of the language. The exact number varies by variety and by researcher — which is itself a sign of how complicated the system is.

And then it collapsed. Fieldwork in 2014 found that most remaining speakers use a single generic classifier for nearly everything, and that the full system now survives mainly in old recordings and in the memory of the oldest speakers.

Cultural significance

Clothes made of salmon

  • A Nivkh summer robe could be sewn from around a hundred pieces of fish skin — salmon, scraped, pounded soft, and stitched with thread cut from more fish skin. It is light, tough and nearly waterproof.
  • The technique was so striking to outsiders that Chinese sources called the peoples of the Amur the "Fish Skin Tatars." An imperial Manchu scroll of 1751 describes their summer clothing in exactly those terms.
  • Almost nothing from a salmon run was wasted. Dried fish fed the sled dogs through winter; skins became clothes and boots; and pounded dried fish mixed with seal fat and berries made mos', a dish still eaten today.
  • The decoration matters too: bold spirals, fish-skin appliqué, dyed birch bark, trade silk from Chinese merchants. Nivkh patterns are recognisably Nivkh and not those of their neighbours.

Every one of these techniques carries its own vocabulary. When a craft stops, a whole region of the dictionary stops with it.

Why fish skin?
On the lower Amur there were no sheep, no cotton and little trade cloth — but there were millions of salmon every year. People built a textile industry out of the one material the river gave them in bulk.

Why dogs?
Dog teams were the winter transport system of the Amur, and dried fish was their fuel. A family's wealth could be counted in dogs — which may be part of why counting things properly mattered so much.

Is it still made?
Yes, mostly by a small number of artists and in museum and festival contexts. Fish-skin work has had a revival across the Amur peoples in recent decades, and it has become one of the most visible markers of Nivkh identity.

The winter ceremony

The bear festival

Held in deep winter, usually January or February, the bear festival was the largest event in Nivkh life — and it was not really about the bear.

  • It was held by relatives to mark the death of a kinsman. A death broke the ties between two clans — the one that gives wives and the one that takes them — and the festival was how those ties were publicly repaired.
  • So the ceremony was simultaneously a funeral rite, a diplomatic summit between clans, a feast, and a message sent to the mountain gods. Shamans presided.
  • Women performed a dance honouring the bear, imitating how it walks, charges and rears — and, in older accounts, wearing fish-skin clothing to do it.

The ceremony involved killing a bear that had been raised in the village, something many students will find hard. It is worth sitting with that rather than skipping it: the ritual treated the animal as an honoured guest being sent home to the gods, which is a genuinely different idea about animals from the one most of us grew up with.

The mechanism

A population that held, a language that didn't

Language loss is usually blamed on something vague — "modernity," "progress." The Nivkh case is unusually clear, because you can name the specific machinery.

  • Collectivization in the 1930s replaced family fishing with wage labour in state enterprises, run in Russian.
  • Village consolidation moved people out of small settlements into larger towns like Nogliki. The small villages were where the language actually lived.
  • Boarding schools took children away from the speakers who would have taught them, and taught them in Russian.
  • Then oil and gas arrived on Sakhalin, bringing an economy that operates entirely in Russian.

Notice that nobody had to ban the language. Four ordinary administrative decisions were enough.

Why it matters now

What exists, and what it would take

  • Nivkh got a written form late. A Latin alphabet was devised around 1931, replaced by Cyrillic, and the modern alphabet was adopted by Soviet decree in 1979, with the writer Vladimir Sangi among its creators.
  • Sangi, born in 1935 in a nomadic settlement on Sakhalin, is the founder of Nivkh literature: primers, school textbooks, orthography rules, and in 2024 a collection of Nivkh legends with an audiobook so people can hear the language as well as read it.
  • A monthly newspaper, Nivkh dif, has published since 1990. The language is taught in the first three school grades in parts of Sakhalin and Khabarovsk Krai.
  • And there is one of the largest online sound archives of any endangered language — recordings of stories and songs, freely available, made with speakers while they were still here.

None of this has yet produced a new generation of children speaking Nivkh at home. That is the honest state of play — and it is the gap every revival project in the world is trying to close.
